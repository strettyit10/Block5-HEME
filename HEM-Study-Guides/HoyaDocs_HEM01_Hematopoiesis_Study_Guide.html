Memory tool — the family tree

The stem cell is the grandparent who can make anyone. Myeloid and lymphoid stem cells are two parents, each able to make only their side of the family. CFUs are the specialists that make one kind of child. Going down the tree, cells lose options and divisions but gain function.

LO 1Normal blood cells and their frequency

Q1. What is blood, as a tissue?

Blood is a highly specialized connective tissue: cells suspended in a liquid matrix (plasma). Its job is transport: O₂ and nutrients to tissues, CO₂ and waste away, cells, hormones and chemical mediators wherever needed, and heat for temperature regulation.

Q2. If you spin a tube of anticoagulated blood, what do you see?

Plasma in one breath

  • 90% water, 10% solutes
  • Albumin = 50–60% of plasma protein; made by the liver; keeps oncotic pressure; carries hydrophobic cargo
  • Globulins (α, β, γ); γ = immunoglobulins from plasma cells
  • Fibrinogen ≈7% → becomes fibrin
  • Also complement, lipoproteins, glucose, amino acids, hormones, electrolytes, gases
CheckWhy is the buffy coat so thin and why does it sit in the middle?
WBCs and platelets are denser than plasma but lighter than packed RBCs, so they settle between them. The layer is only ≈1% because RBCs vastly outnumber them (millions/mm³ vs thousands).
Memory tool

Plasma − fibrinogen = serum. Let blood clot, fibrinogen is used up, the leftover fluid is serum.

In-house exam

Know the order and the numbers (60–70 / 28 / 5 / 2–4 / 0.5) and the one-word job of each: neutrophil → bacteria; lymphocyte → humoral and cellular immunity; monocyte → becomes phagocytic macrophage; eosinophil → parasites; basophil → inflammation. Q7. How do I recognize each mature cell, and what does it do?

Erythrocyte RBC

Most numerous cell · 120 days
  • Biconcave disc, 8 µm × 2 µm; no nucleus, no mitochondria, no ribosomes → no protein synthesis
  • Squeezes through 3–4 µm capillaries; withstands 5× serum osmotic pressure
  • Energy = anaerobic glycolysis
  • Removed by splenic macrophages → Fe + bilirubin

Neutrophil (PMN) 60–70%

6 h in blood · 1–4 days in tissue
  • 2–5 lobes; pale salmon-pink cytoplasm
  • Drumstick in ≈3% of female PMNs = inactive X (Barr body)
  • Kills bacteria and fungi; leads acute inflammation

Eosinophil 2–4%

Lives in GI, respiratory, lower GU tissue
  • Bilobed nucleus + large orange-red granules
  • Kills parasites (helminths); "damage control" in allergy

Basophil 0.5%

Rarest WBC
  • Dark blue-purple granules hide a 2–3-lobed nucleus
  • IgE on surface → histamine, heparin release; inflammation, recruits eosinophils

Lymphocyte 28%

Days to years
  • Small (8–9 µm ≈ RBC); round dark nucleus, thin rim of blue cytoplasm
  • Activated: up to 16 µm
  • Only WBC that recirculates, can still divide, lives years

Monocyte 5%

40 h in blood · months in tissue
  • Largest WBC; eccentric kidney/horseshoe nucleus, not lobed; 2–3 nucleoli
  • Grey-blue "frosted glass" cytoplasm, vacuoles
  • Becomes macrophage; does not re-enter blood

Platelet 150–450k

10 days · ⅓ stored in spleen
  • 2–3 µm fragment; no nucleus but has mitochondria
  • Pale hyalomere rim + purple granulomere center
  • Clump on smears; stick at once to subendothelial collagen
Deep dive · Erythrocyte structure
  • Membrane skeleton: spectrin + actin lattice, attached to the membrane by ankyrin → keeps the biconcave shape, resists shear
  • Membrane proteins: glycophorin (transmembrane glycoprotein), band 3 (anion channel, Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻); carbohydrate groups set the ABO blood type and their negative charge prevents clumping
  • Hemoglobin: 4 heme groups (iron = O₂-binding site, porphyrin ring) + globin tetramer. Adults: HbA ≈97%, HbA2 2–3%, HbF <1%
  • Reticulocyte: new RBC with residual rRNA for 1–2 days; looks polychromatophilic (bluish) on Wright stain; supravital stain (brilliant cresyl blue) shows the reticulum
Step 1
  • Spectrin / ankyrin / band 3 defect → hereditary spherocytosis (no central pallor, ↑ MCHC, splenomegaly).
  • No mitochondria → total dependence on glycolysis → pyruvate kinase deficiency causes hemolysis. 10% of glucose goes to the HMP shunt → G6PD deficiency.
  • Neonatal RBC lifespan is only 60–90 days.

Getting to the infection

  1. Margination: rolling (selectins) and sticking (integrins)
  2. Diapedesis between endothelial cells
  3. Chemotaxis toward chemokines from inflamed tissue

Killing the microbe

  1. Recognition via receptors
  2. Phagocytosis → phagosome; much better if microbe is opsonized (antibody or complement)
  3. O₂-dependent: respiratory burst → O₂⁻ → H₂O₂ → MPO makes HOCl (bleach)
  4. O₂-independent: acidified phagosome + granule enzymes
  • Few mitochondria, abundant glycogen → works in low-O₂ infected tissue
  • Pools: 50% circulating, 50% marginated (stuck to endothelium, esp. lung) + a marrow reserve 10× the blood amount
Step 1
  • NADPH oxidase defect → chronic granulomatous disease. MPO deficiency → mostly benign, ↑ Candida.
  • Neutrophil chemotactic agents: C5a, IL-8, LTB4, 5-HETE, kallikrein, PAF, N-formylmethionine.
  • Glucocorticoids → demargination → ↑ neutrophil count without infection; ↓ eosinophils and lymphocytes.
  • Toxic granulation and Döhle bodies = reactive neutrophils in infection. Leukoerythroblastic reaction = left shift + immature RBCs → marrow infiltration (myelofibrosis, metastasis).
Deep dive · Eosinophil and basophil granules

Eosinophil specific granule

  • Externum (outer, less dense): peroxidase, hydrolytic lysosomal enzymes
  • Internum (crystalloid core):
    • Major basic protein: most abundant; anti-parasitic; gives the orange color
    • Eosinophil cationic protein: most potent tissue-damaging protein
    • Eosinophil-derived neurotoxin
  • Anti-inflammatory: phagocytoses Ag–Ab complexes; neutralizes mast cell / basophil mediators
  • Drawn in by eosinophil chemotactic factor from mast cells and basophils

Basophil (vs mast cell)

  • Preformed: histamine, proteases, heparin (causes the basophilia), ECF
  • Made on demand after activation: leukotrienes
  • IgE held on surface receptors → antigen cross-links → release
  • Mast cells: similar granules but round/oval nucleus, live in tissue, more but smaller granules
Step 1
  • Causes of eosinophilia — PACMAN Eats: Parasites, Asthma, Chronic adrenal insufficiency, Myeloproliferative disorders, Allergic processes, Neoplasia (Hodgkin lymphoma), Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis.
  • Basophilia is uncommon → think CML.
  • Mast cell: c-KIT mutation → mastocytosis (↑ tryptase). Vancomycin, opioids, radiocontrast → IgE-independent degranulation. Cromolyn blocks degranulation. Mepolizumab blocks IL-5.
Deep dive · Platelets and the six steps of hemostasis

Platelet anatomy

  • Hyalomere: outer, light blue; actin–myosin + marginal bundle of microtubules (keeps the disc)
  • Granulomere: central purple; granules ≈20% of volume
    • Dense: serotonin, ADP, Ca²⁺
    • Alpha: PF4, vWF, fibrinogen, PDGF
    • Lambda: lysosomal hydrolases
  • Canalicular system (releases granules); dense tubular system
  • Membrane glycoproteins (integrins) bind vWF and fibrinogen

Clot formation in order

  1. Reflex vasoconstriction
  2. Adhesion to subendothelial collagen via vWF (seconds)
  3. Activation & release of mediators; disc → sphere with pseudopods; thromboxane made
  4. Aggregation via fibrinogen → primary plug (primary hemostasis)
  5. Fibrin from thrombin → firm plug over hours (secondary hemostasis)
  6. Clot retraction (actin, myosin, ATP)
Step 1

vWF receptor = GpIb (defect: Bernard-Soulier). Fibrinogen receptor = GpIIb/IIIa (defect: Glanzmann). Dense granules = CASH (Ca²⁺, ADP, Serotonin, Histamine). Splenomegaly → more platelets pooled → thrombocytopenia.

Step 1 — which cell is up?
  • ↑ Neutrophils + bands (left shift) → bacterial infection; steroids; stress

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A child from an endemic area has abdominal pain. WBC 12,500 with 18% of a cell that has a bilobed nucleus and large orange-red granules. Which granule protein does this cell rely on for its main defense role?
Major basic protein in the crystalloid internum. This is an eosinophil (normal 2–4%) responding to a helminth. MPO and lactoferrin belong to neutrophils; histamine and heparin to basophils.
A2A febrile patient with lobar pneumonia has WBC 18,000 with many neutrophils whose nucleus is a single unsegmented U shape. What are they and what do they mean?
Band cells → left shift. The marrow releases its reserve pool early. Bands are normally ≤3%; >10% suggests infection or inflammation.
A3A woman starts high-dose prednisone and two days later her WBC rises from 7,000 to 13,000 with no fever. Mechanism?
Demargination. ≈50% of neutrophils are stuck to vessel walls; steroids release them into the circulating pool.
A4Which WBC can leave the blood, enter tissue, and come back through lymphatics?
Lymphocyte. It also keeps the ability to divide and can survive for years.
A5A cirrhotic patient with portal hypertension has platelets of 70,000 with a normal marrow. Why?
Splenic sequestration. Normally up to ⅓ of platelets sit in the red pulp; a big, congested spleen holds far more. Liver failure also lowers TPO.

LO 2Myeloid vs lymphoid split

Q1. Why does the body need hematopoiesis at all?

Hematopoiesis is the production of blood cells. Every blood cell has a finite lifespan (neutrophil ≈6 h in blood, platelet ≈10 days, RBC ≈120 days), so cells must be replaced continuously, under the control of growth factors.

Q2. What is the pluripotent hematopoietic stem cell (PHSC)?
FeatureWhat to know
PotentialPluripotent ("hemocytoblast"): can become any blood cell, lymphocytes included. Myeloid and lymphoid stem cells are multipotent and self-renewing.
MorphologyNo distinguishing features — cannot be identified by looking at it
How it was provenInject marrow cells into lethally irradiated mice → donor cells form spleen colonies and rescue the animal
MarkersCD34+ and c-kit (receptor for stem cell factor) by flow cytometry
Frequency≈0.01% of nucleated marrow cells; only ≈5% of normal stem cells are needed to repopulate the whole marrow
ActivityMostly not dividing (quiescent) → protects them from depletion. Daily output comes from committed progenitors dividing.
Where elseSmall numbers in blood; large numbers in umbilical cord blood
CheckIf stem cells mostly don't divide, where do billions of new cells per day come from?
From the committed (restricted) progenitors and the early, still-dividing precursors. The stem cell divides only now and then to keep its own pool full.
Step 1

CD34 is how transplant centers count and collect stem cells. Donors receive G-CSF (filgrastim) to mobilize CD34+ cells into blood. CD34 also marks blasts in acute leukemia.

Memory tool

Lymphoid = the "BTNK club" (B, T, NK + plasma cells). Myeloid = everyone else in blood.

Trap

Monocytes are myeloid, even though they are "mononuclear" agranulocytes like lymphocytes. Neutrophils and monocytes are siblings from CFU-GM. "Agranulocyte" is a smear word (LO 1); "lymphoid" is a lineage word (LO 2).

Q4. What tells each progenitor what to become? (growth factors)
  • Bone marrow stromal cells make stem cell factor, which stem cells need to survive. No growth factor → apoptosis.
  • Growth factors bind specific receptors and control proliferation, differentiation and activation. They act endocrine (EPO from kidney) or paracrine (local marrow signals).
  • Committed progenitors are named for the colony they form in culture: CFU = colony-forming unit.

Step 1 — growth factors as drugs
  • Epoetin / darbepoetin (EPO; signals through JAK2/STAT) → anemia of CKD, chemo anemia. Watch: hypertension, thrombosis; abuse by athletes.
  • Filgrastim (G-CSF) → chemo neutropenia, stem cell mobilization. Sargramostim (GM-CSF) → marrow recovery.
  • Romiplostim, eltrombopag (TPO receptor agonists) → ITP.
  • Hypoxia → ↑ EPO → secondary polycythemia; EPO-secreting tumors (renal cell carcinoma, HCC, hemangioblastoma) → inappropriate polycythemia.
In-house exam

Know: renal insufficiency → ↓ EPO → anemia; EPO stimulates erythroid progenitor proliferation/differentiation and inhibits apoptosis; rhEPO cut transfusions in dialysis by 70–80%; rhGM-CSF helps leukopenic patients (BMT, cancer therapy, AIDS, aplastic anemia); TPO agonists treat ITP.

Q7. What does the marrow look like up close? ("seeds in the soil")
CheckWhy do megakaryocytes sit on the sinus wall while granulocyte precursors sit far away?
Megakaryocytes are huge (≈100 µm) and can't move, so they park on the abluminal surface of the sinus and shed platelets straight into the blood. Granulocyte precursors become motile at the metamyelocyte stage, so they can mature far away and crawl to the sinus when ready.
Analogy — seeds in the soil

Stem cells are seeds; they only grow in the right soil, the Hematopoietic Inductive Microenvironment (HIM): stromal cells + reticular fibers (scaffold), adhesion molecules (let stem cells "home" back to the marrow, which is why IV-infused transplant cells find their way) and growth factors (stem cell factor). Different lineages grow in different "beds" of the garden.

Q9. How does the tree help me localize disease?
High yield — count the lineages
  • All lines low (pancytopenia) → stem cell / whole marrow. Hypocellular fatty marrow = aplastic anemia.
  • Myeloid lines high → myeloid stem cell (myeloproliferative neoplasm, CML).
  • One line abnormal → its progenitor, its growth factor, or the periphery (CKD → only RBCs fall).
  • Leukemia = clone blocked at one stage; the blocked branch names it (AML vs ALL).
In-house exam — classic item style

"Based on the abnormal CBC finding, which hematopoietic cell is most likely to show a quantitative abnormality in the marrow?" Match the abnormal count to its precursor: ↑ platelets → megakaryocyte; ↑ RBC → proerythroblast line; ↑ granulocytes → myelocyte line.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A sibling donor receives 5 days of injections; stem cells are collected from blood and purified for one surface marker. Which drug and marker?
G-CSF (filgrastim) to mobilize; select CD34+ cells. PHSCs have no distinguishing morphology and make up only ≈0.01% of marrow cells.
A2A man on hemodialysis has Hb 8.1, normal MCV and low reticulocytes. Which cell normally makes the missing hormone, and which progenitor responds to low levels of it?
Kidney peritubular interstitial cells (≈90% of EPO). CFU-E responds to low EPO; BFU-E needs high EPO.
A3A boy with β-thalassemia major has frontal bossing and a liver 6 cm below the costal margin; biopsy shows nucleated red precursors. When was this organ normally the main site of blood formation?
The 2nd trimester (hepato-spleno-thymic stage). This is extramedullary hematopoiesis from prolonged, severe demand.
A4Pancytopenia after chloramphenicol. What will the marrow biopsy show?
Hypocellular marrow replaced by fat → aplastic anemia (stem cell failure).
A5CML (t(9;22)) can turn into a lymphoid blast crisis. What does that tell you about the cell that first carried BCR-ABL?
It was the pluripotent stem cell, above the myeloid/lymphoid split.

"Shrink, condense, lose the blue." As a precursor matures it shrinks, its chromatin condenses, its nucleoli vanish, and its cytoplasm loses the blue ribosomes as it fills with its final product.

Erythropoiesis The RBC line

Q2. What are the erythroid stages, and how do I tell them apart?

EPO acts on BFU-E → CFU-E. Recognizable maturation takes ≈7 days and happens in erythroblastic islands. The first three stages divide; from the normoblast on, no more mitosis.

Memory tool — order and color

"Please Be Polite, Or Run Early" → Pro → Basophilic → Polychromatophilic → Orthochromatophilic (normoblast) → Reticulocyte → Erythrocyte.

Color story: blue → blue + pink → pink. "Poly-chromato-philic" = loves many colors. "Ortho-chromato-philic" = the correct (RBC) color.

In-house exam

Two erythroid photo items appear in the lecture's own practice set: identify the polychromatophilic erythroblast (clue: polychromatophilia) and "which cell precedes it?" (basophilic erythroblast). Also identify the normoblast: pyknotic, eccentric nucleus in a cell about the size of an RBC. The normoblast is incapable of mitosis.

Step 1 — erythroid line
  • Reticulocytes separate anemias: ↑ = marrow responding (hemolysis, bleeding); ↓ = marrow problem (aplasia, iron/B12/folate lack, CKD). Normal ≈0.5–1.5%; correct with the reticulocyte production index.
  • Howell-Jolly bodies = nuclear remnants the spleen normally removes → asplenia.
  • Basophilic stippling = clumped ribosomes → lead poisoning, thalassemia.
  • Megaloblastic anemia (B12/folate): DNA lags while Hb fills → nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony.
  • Parvovirus B19 infects erythroid progenitors → aplastic crisis in sickle cell or spherocytosis.
  • Nucleated RBCs in blood → marrow stress or infiltration (severe hemolysis, myelofibrosis).

Granulopoiesis The granulocyte line

Q3. What are the granulocytic stages, and what is the key feature of each?

GM-CSF acts on CFU-GM, CFU-Eo and CFU-Bas. Maturation takes ≈11 days; neutrophils then wait ≈4 days in the marrow reserve before release. Watch two stories at once: the nucleus (round → indented → horseshoe → lobes) and the granules (none → azurophilic → specific).

Memory tools

"My Parents Made Me Be Smart" → Myeloblast → Promyelocyte → Myelocyte → Metamyelocyte → Band → Segmented.

Primary granules appear in the Promyelocyte; Secondary (Specific) granules in the myelocyte.

"Meta" means after: once a cell is a metamyelocyte it has stopped dividing and started moving.

Traps
  • Metamyelocyte vs band: kidney bean (a dent) vs horseshoe/U (deep curve, even width).
  • Promyelocyte vs myelocyte: promyelocyte is bigger with only purple granules; myelocyte is smaller with specific granules (pink, orange or blue).
  • Largest cell of granulopoiesis = promyelocyte. Largest cell in the marrow = megakaryocyte.
In-house exam

Practice-set items: name this granulopoiesis cell (myelocyte), identify the kidney-bean cell (metamyelocyte), and "which cell precedes this one?" shown a band (answer: metamyelocyte). Also: "excess band neutrophils in blood may indicate a bacterial infection."

Step 1 — granulocytic line
  • Acute promyelocytic leukemia: cells stuck at the promyelocyte, t(15;17) PML-RARα, many Auer rods (fused azurophilic granules), DIC; treat with ATRA.
  • Left shift (↑ bands, metamyelocytes) → infection or CML. Leukemoid reaction has high LAP; CML has low LAP and basophilia.
  • Hypersegmented neutrophils (≥6 lobes) → B12 or folate deficiency.

Monopoiesis The monocyte line

Q4. How does a monocyte develop, and what does it become?
1 · Monoblast
Large; scant–moderate basophilic cytoplasm; large nucleus with distinct/prominent nucleoli. Virtually identical to a myeloblast.
mitotic
2 · Promonocyte
Folded nucleus with delicate creases and an indent. First identifiable cell of the line.
mitotic
3 · Monocyte
Irregular nucleus from a small indent to early lobulation; grey-blue cytoplasm.
postmitotic
  • Comes from CFU-GM, the same progenitor as the neutrophil
  • Mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) = macrophages, their precursors (incl. monocytes) and specialized descendants (e.g., giant cells)
  • Macrophage jobs: phagocytosis (intracellular bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, debris), secretion (IL-1, TNF), antigen presentation to lymphocytes
Step 1

Macrophages by tissue: Kupffer (liver), microglia (CNS), osteoclasts (bone), histiocytes (connective tissue), alveolar macrophages; Langerhans cells (skin dendritic cells). Activated by IFN-γ; present on MHC II; LPS binds CD14 → septic shock. Fuse into giant cells in granulomas (TB, sarcoid).

Thrombopoiesis The platelet line

Q5. How does one cell make thousands of platelets?

TPO regulates the maturation, size and number of megakaryocytes (via CFU-Meg). The trick is endomitosis: the nucleus copies its DNA without the cell dividing, giving one giant polyploid nucleus (2n up to 64n).

1 · Megakaryoblast
Very large for a marrow cell (8–30 µm); basophilic cytoplasm; endomitosis begins.
2 · Promegakaryocyte
Larger still; multiple-lobed nucleus; basophilic cytoplasm.
3 · Megakaryocyte
Largest marrow cell (~100 µm); multilobed polyploid nucleus; cytoplasm shedding platelets.
4 · Platelets
Pinched off along demarcation channels; hyalomere + granulomere.
  • Megakaryocytes sit on the abluminal surface of sinusoids and extend proplatelets through the endothelium
  • Some enter the blood and finish making platelets in the lung capillaries (seen in pulmonary cytology)
Trap

Megakaryocytes break the "cells shrink as they mature" rule: they grow. A giant marrow cell with one lobulated nucleus = megakaryocyte; a giant cell with many separate nuclei on bone = osteoclast.

Step 1

Marrow megakaryocytes + low platelets: ↑ megakaryocytes → peripheral destruction (ITP, anti-GpIIb/IIIa); ↓ megakaryocytes → production failure. Essential thrombocythemia → many atypical megakaryocytes (JAK2, CALR, MPL). Liver failure → ↓ TPO.

Lymphopoiesis The lymphocyte line

Q6. What do lymphoid cells look like on the way to maturity?
1 · Lymphoblast
10–20 µm; condensed chromatin; no cytoplasmic granules.
mitotic
2 · Prolymphocyte
Prominent nucleolus; condensed chromatin; moderate pale, weakly basophilic cytoplasm.
3 · Lymphocyte (B or T)
Condensed round/oval nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm. B and T look identical.
Plasma cell (from B)
Eccentric nucleus; abundant cytoplasm full of RER and Golgi; makes antibody.
NK cell
Large granular lymphocyte; kills virus-infected and early cancer cells (MHC I recognition).
Step 1

B cells: CD19, CD20, CD21; mature in marrow. T cells: CD3 (CD4 or CD8); mature in thymus; ≈80% of circulating lymphocytes. NK: CD16, CD56; perforin + granzymes; kill cells with ↓ MHC I. Plasma cell: clock-face chromatin, perinuclear halo (Golgi); normally does not circulate; malignant in multiple myeloma. ALL = lymphoblasts, TdT+.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Gum bleeding, oozing IV sites, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer. Marrow full of large cells packed with purple granules and rod-shaped inclusions. Cytogenetics?
t(15;17) — acute promyelocytic leukemia. Cells stuck at the promyelocyte (azurophilic granules); Auer rods; granule contents trigger DIC; ATRA lets them mature.
A2A child with sickle cell has a sudden Hb drop from 8 to 4 g/dL with reticulocytes 0.1% after a "slapped cheek" rash. What does the virus target?
Erythroid progenitors (parvovirus B19). Production stops for about a week; with short RBC survival, that causes an aplastic crisis.
A3A splenectomy patient's RBCs contain single small dark purple inclusions. What are they?
Howell-Jolly bodies: nuclear remnants left after the normoblast ejects its nucleus, normally removed by the spleen.
A4Isolated platelets of 15,000 with normal Hb and WBC; marrow shows increased megakaryocytes. Explanation?
Peripheral destruction (ITP). The marrow compensates by making more megakaryocytes.
A5Macrocytic anemia; marrow erythroid precursors are oversized with immature, fine chromatin but already pink cytoplasm. Which process is impaired?
DNA synthesis (B12/folate) → nucleus lags behind cytoplasm: nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony.

MCQExam-style questions

Score: 0 / 0

1A 64-year-old woman has fatigue and a ruddy complexion. CBC: Hb 12.5 g/dL, Hct 36%, WBC 8,500/mm³, platelets 580,000/mm³. Based on the abnormal finding, which hematopoietic cell is most likely to show a quantitative abnormality in her marrow?

Only the platelets are abnormal (high). Platelets come from megakaryocytes, so that is the marrow population that will be increased. Match the abnormal count to its precursor.

2A 62-year-old man with CKD from long-standing diabetes has fatigue. Hb 9.5 g/dL, Hct 25%, normocytic normochromic RBCs, low reticulocytes. A hormone made by renal interstitial cells is deficient. Which process is most directly impaired?

EPO stimulates proliferation and differentiation of erythroid progenitors and blocks their apoptosis. CKD → ↓ EPO → hypoproliferative anemia with low retics.

3A 6-year-old with mild fever has a normal WBC count, but bands make up 12% of the differential. Which description fits the stage immediately before the elevated cell?

The band is preceded by the metamyelocyte (kidney-bean nucleus). A = proerythroblast; B = normoblast; D = plasma cell; E = megakaryocyte line.

4A marrow aspirate from a healthy donor shows a central macrophage surrounded by developing erythroid cells at different stages. What is the central cell's main job?

The erythroblastic island's "nurse" macrophage supplies iron, supports growth and eats the nuclei ejected by normoblasts.

5In a granulocyte precursor, which is the first stage at which a neutrophil can be distinguished from an eosinophil?

Specific (secondary) granules appear in the myelocyte, and their color tells the lineage. The promyelocyte has only non-specific azurophilic granules.

6Which erythroid stage is the last one capable of mitosis?

Pro, basophilic and polychromatophilic erythroblasts divide. From the normoblast (orthochromatophilic) on, cells are postmitotic.

7Researchers inject purified marrow cells into lethally irradiated mice; the cells form spleen colonies and rescue the animals. The cells have no distinctive morphology. Which marker would identify them?

Pluripotent stem cells are CD34+ (and c-kit+). CD3 = T cell; CD19 = B cell; CD56 = NK; MPO = myeloid granules.

8A smear shows a cell with a dark blue-purple granule mass that obscures a bilobed nucleus. Which of the following is stored in these granules and responsible for their staining?

Basophil. Heparin gives the granules their basophilia; histamine, proteases and ECF are also stored. Leukotrienes are made on demand, not stored.

9A 3-year-old needs a bone marrow aspirate. Which site is preferred?

Young children → tibia. Adults → pelvis (iliac crest) or sternum.

10A woman's neutrophils show a small "drumstick" appendage on the nucleus in about 3% of cells. This represents:

The drumstick is the Barr body (inactive X), a normal finding in female neutrophils.

11A 30-year-old with pancytopenia has a bone marrow biopsy. Which finding best fits aplastic anemia?

Aplastic anemia = stem cell failure → fatty, "empty" hypocellular marrow. B = acute leukemia; E = myelofibrosis.

12Which mature cell shares its committed progenitor (CFU-GM) with the neutrophil?

Neutrophils and monocytes both come from CFU-GM. Monocytes are myeloid despite being "mononuclear."
