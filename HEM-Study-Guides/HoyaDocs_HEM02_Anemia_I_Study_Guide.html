LO 1Defining anemia

Q1. What does a normal red cell look like, and how is it removed?
  • Biconcave disc; made in marrow under EPO (from the kidney); average lifespan 120 days
  • Membrane: spectrin + actin lattice gives strength and flexibility. Shape changes with flow: fast arterial flow → asymmetric "slipper"; slow venous flow → symmetric "bullet"
  • Removal:
    • Old (senescent) cells → eaten by macrophages
    • Stiff (unpliable) cells → trapped in the spleen
  • Recycling: iron → stored, returned to marrow · porphyrin → bilirubin → bile · globin → amino acid pool
Q2. What is hemoglobin, and which types are normal?
  • Found only in RBCs. Carries O₂ from lungs → tissues and CO₂ + H⁺ from tissues → lungs
  • Heme = porphyrin ring with central ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), the O₂-binding site · Globin = protein chains
  • 4 heme + 4 globin chains → one Hb carries up to 4 O₂
HemoglobinChainsAdultsNewborns
HbAα₂β₂≈95%≈20%
HbA1cα₂β₂ + glucose<3.5%—
HbA2α₂δ₂<2.5%<0.5%

Memory tool

Fetal → adult: "Alpha always; gamma goes, becomes beta." Every normal Hb has α chains.

Trap

β-chain diseases (β-thalassemia, sickle cell) are silent at birth because the newborn runs on HbF (α₂γ₂), which needs no β chains. Symptoms start around 6 months as HbF falls.

Step 1

HbF binds 2,3-BPG less → higher O₂ affinity → pulls O₂ across the placenta. ↑ HbF is the goal of hydroxyurea in sickle cell.

Q3. So what exactly is anemia?

A reduction in the total number of red blood cells, or a reduction in the circulating red cell mass. In practice: a low Hb or Hct.

Normal balanceValue
Production vs lossUnder normal conditions, production = loss
RBC lifespan110–120 days
Removed per day≈1% of RBCs
Retics made per day≈50,000 /µL of blood
Marrow reserveCan raise output ≈5-fold in response to EPO
Q4. What are the only three ways to become anemic?
  1. Decreased RBC production (iron, B12/folate lack, marrow failure, low EPO)
  2. Increased RBC destruction (hemolysis: sickle cell, G6PD, spherocytosis, autoimmune)
  3. Blood loss (acute bleeding, chronic GI or menstrual loss)
Analogy — the bathtub

Red cell mass is the water level. The faucet is the marrow (production); the drain is destruction; a crack in the tub is bleeding. Anemia means the faucet is too slow, the drain too fast, or the tub is leaking. The reticulocyte count tells you whether the faucet is working.

Trap

Right after a large acute bleed, Hb can still be normal because whole blood is lost (cells + plasma together). Judge acute blood loss by vital signs, not the first Hb.

In-house exam

Know the definition word for word in spirit: reduced total RBC number or circulating RBC mass, and the three causes: decreased production, increased destruction, blood loss.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A healthy adult's marrow suddenly stops making red cells. Roughly what fraction of her red cells would be gone after 10 days?
About 10%. ≈1% of RBCs are removed each day (120-day lifespan), so a 10-day shutdown drops the mass ≈10%. That is why a brief stop (parvovirus B19) only matters when RBC survival is already short.
A2Which of the three mechanisms produces anemia with a high reticulocyte count?
Increased destruction (hemolysis) and blood loss: the marrow is healthy and speeds up. Decreased production gives a low reticulocyte count.

In-house exam

This course defines microcytic as MCV <70 fL. Use <70 on the block exam and <80 on boards.

Q3. How do size and mechanism fit together?

Size points to the cause; the reticulocyte count points to the mechanism. Use both.

  • Reticulocyte count = newborn RBCs; shows whether the marrow is answering the anemia
  • Low MCV + low retics → production problem (iron deficiency)
  • Normal MCV + high retics → destruction or bleeding (sickle cell, hemolysis)
  • High MCV + high retics → hemolysis (retics are big); high MCV + low retics → B12/folate, marrow problem
Step 1 — reticulocyte production index

The raw retic % is falsely high in anemia, so correct it: RPI = retic % × (patient Hct ÷ normal Hct) ÷ maturation time.

RPI >3 → marrow compensating (hemolysis, bleeding). RPI <2 → inadequate response (production problem).

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1Hb 8, MCV 92, retics 9%, ↑ indirect bilirubin. Which group by size and by mechanism?
Normocytic, increased destruction (hemolysis). High retics and bilirubin show a working marrow and red cells breaking down.
A2Hb 9, MCV 64, retics 0.5%. Which group and mechanism?
Microcytic, decreased production. Think iron deficiency first.

LO 3Common causes by cell size

Q1. Why are some anemias microcytic, and which are the common ones?

Microcytic cells have normal DNA synthesis but too little hemoglobin. Precursors keep dividing while waiting for enough Hb, so they end up small and pale. Any defect in iron supply, heme synthesis or globin synthesis does it.

CauseDefectKey features
Iron deficiencyNot enough ironMost common anemia; almost always microcytic with a high RDW; malnutrition or chronic blood loss
ThalassemiasNot enough globinUsually microcytic (can be normocytic or macrocytic from hemolysis); normal RDW; target cells
SideroblasticHeme synthesis; iron trapped in erythroid precursorsHereditary, MDS, lead, alcohol, drugs; ringed sideroblasts
Anemia of chronic diseaseIron trapped in marrow macrophagesChronic inflammation; may be normocytic
In-house exam — clinical tips
  • Low Hb/Hct → look at the MCV. Low MCV → think iron deficiency first (RDW usually high).
  • Next, the smear: sickle cells → sickle cell anemia; target cells → thalassemia or sickle cell; RBC fragments → intravascular hemolysis.
  • Suspected iron deficiency in a child → treat; in an adult → confirm with iron studies.
  • No cause found and chronic disease or sideroblastic anemia suspected → bone marrow evaluation.
Q2. Which anemias are macrocytic?
  • Megaloblastic (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency): cells are large and oval; B12 is the most common cause
  • Chronic alcohol use
  • Aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia
  • Some hemolytic anemias (many large reticulocytes)
  • Decreased marrow function with low retics: drugs, toxins, liver and thyroid disease

Work-up: smear (megaloblastic changes?) + reticulocyte count. High retics → hemolysis or bleeding. Normal/low retics → consider marrow evaluation.

Step 1

Megaloblastic = hypersegmented neutrophils (≥6 lobes) + oval macrocytes. B12 deficiency adds ↑ methylmalonic acid and neuro signs; folate does not. (Full detail in Anemia II.)

Q3. What should I think of with a normocytic anemia?
  • Acute bleeding
  • Sickle cell disease
  • Anemia of chronic disease
  • Myelophthisic (infiltrative) marrow: metastatic tumor, myelofibrosis, infection in children
  • Primary hematologic malignancy (leukemia, lymphoma) crowding out normal marrow

Normocytic, normochromic anemia usually needs a bone marrow evaluation; normocytic + high retics → work up like hemolysis.

Trap

Iron deficiency (small cells) + B12/folate deficiency (big cells) can average to a normal MCV with a very high RDW (celiac disease, malnutrition, after gastric bypass).

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 4-year-old with pica has an MCV of 62. Before giving iron, which other microcytic cause should a basophilic-stippled smear make you consider?
Lead poisoning (a sideroblastic cause). Pica is common to both.
A2An MCV of 112 with a reticulocyte count of 12%. Most likely group of causes?
Hemolysis (or recovery from bleeding): large reticulocytes raise the MCV.

Memory tool

MCV = size. RDW = variety. "V" for volume, "W" for width of the size spread.

Step 1 — Mentzer index

MCV ÷ RBC count (millions/µL). <13 → thalassemia trait (many small cells, RBC count normal or high). >13 → iron deficiency (fewer cells).

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1Two patients have MCV 68. Patient A: RBC 3.9 million, RDW 19%. Patient B: RBC 5.8 million, RDW 13%. Which is which?
A: Mentzer 68 ÷ 3.9 ≈ 17 (>13) + high RDW → iron deficiency. B: 68 ÷ 5.8 ≈ 11.7 (<13) + normal RDW → thalassemia trait.
A2A resident orders a bone marrow biopsy as the first test for a mild isolated anemia. Better first steps?
CBC indices (MCV, RDW), reticulocyte count and a peripheral smear. Marrow biopsy is often not helpful for isolated anemia.

LO 5Signs and symptoms

Q1. What symptoms does any anemia cause?

All come from less O₂ delivered to tissues plus the body's compensation:

  • Fatigue, weakness, dizziness, headache
  • Shortness of breath and chest pain on exertion (angina if coronary disease)
  • Palpitations, tachycardia, flow murmur
Step 1

Compensation: ↑ cardiac output (can cause high-output heart failure in severe chronic anemia), ↑ 2,3-BPG (right shift → easier O₂ unloading), ↑ EPO.

Trap

Speed matters more than depth. A slow drop (iron deficiency, thal trait) can reach a very low Hb with few symptoms; a fast drop of the same size is poorly tolerated.

Q2. What do you see on exam?

Pallor of skin and mucous membranes, best seen in the conjunctiva, palms and palmar creases, and nail beds.

Palmar pallor

  • Pale palm and creases next to a normal hand

Conjunctival pallor

  • Pull down the lower lid; pale inner rim
Q3. Which findings hint at a specific cause?
FindingThink
Jaundice, splenomegaly, dark urineHemolysis
Pica, koilonychia, blue scleraIron deficiency
Bony deformities, hepatosplenomegaly in a childβ-thalassemia major
Pain crises, leg ulcersSickle cell disease
Neuropathy, glossitisB12 deficiency
In-house exam

A complete history and physical exam are the most valuable tools in diagnosing anemia; clinical suspicion guides which tests to order.

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A 70-year-old with Hb 7 reports chest pressure walking upstairs. Why?
Less O₂-carrying capacity + higher heart rate raise myocardial O₂ demand → demand ischemia (exertional angina).
A2A fatigued patient with anemia has yellow sclerae and a palpable spleen tip. Which mechanism?
Increased destruction (hemolysis): bilirubin from broken-down heme; spleen working hard.

LO 6Iron deficiency anemia

Q1. How does the body normally handle iron?

TransferrinPlasma iron carrier; protects tissues from free iron; delivers to cells with transferrin receptors (marrow, liver, muscle)Measured as TIBC; normally ≈33% saturated

Step 1

Fe²⁺ enters via DMT1 and leaves the enterocyte via ferroportin. Hepcidin (made by the liver, ↑ by IL-6 in inflammation) degrades ferroportin → iron trapped in enterocytes and macrophages = anemia of chronic disease. Vitamin C helps absorption.

Q2. Who gets iron deficiency, and why? (causes by age)

The most common cause of anemia; most common in toddlers, adolescent girls and women of childbearing age. Four mechanisms, often more than one at once:

MechanismExamples
Inadequate intakeInfants on a milk-only diet; picky toddlers; elderly with restricted diets or poor dentition; vegetarians
Impaired absorptionTea, wine, bran/high-fiber diets; drugs that raise gastric pH (PPIs); duodenal surgery or resection; celiac disease; some infections
Increased requirementGrowing children, pregnancy, menstruating women, blood donors
Chronic blood lossGI tract (peptic ulcer, gastritis, colon carcinoma), heavy menses
PatientMost likely cause
Infant / toddlerDiet (milk) + rapid growth
Premenopausal womanMenstrual loss (GI cancer in only ≈1%)
Man or postmenopausal womanOccult GI blood loss → GI cancer found in 8–10%
Adult >65Colon or other GI carcinoma until proven otherwise
High yield

In every adult with iron deficiency, consider occult GI blood loss and look with endoscopy and/or colonoscopy. Dual pathology (upper + lower GI) is found in 1–10%, especially in older patients.

Step 1

Right-sided colon cancer → occult bleeding → iron deficiency anemia in an older adult.

Q3. What clinical clues are unique to iron deficiency?

General anemia symptoms (fatigue) plus clues from iron's role in many enzymes:

Koilonychia

  • Thin, brittle, spoon-shaped nails
  • Pica: craving inert things (ice, chalk, dirt, starch). Craving ice = pagophagia, very suggestive
  • Blue sclera: thin sclera (rarely seen)
  • Children: motor and mental developmental delay
Step 1

Also: restless legs, glossitis, angular cheilitis, and Plummer-Vinson syndrome (iron deficiency + esophageal webs + dysphagia).

Q4. Which tests confirm it, and what do they show?

Labs

  • ↓ Hb, ↓ Hct
  • ↓ MCV (microcytic)
  • ↑ RDW (many sizes)
  • ↓ Serum iron (iron bound to transferrin)
  • ↑ TIBC (more transferrin, hungry for iron)
  • ↓ Serum ferritin (empty stores) — key test
  • ↓ transferrin saturation
  • Smear: microcytic, hypochromic, marked anisocytosis/poikilocytosis, pencil cells
  • Marrow iron stores: absent

In-house exam

Classic item: heavy menses from fibroids, MCV 68, ferritin 5, TIBC 500 → smear shows poikilocytosis (variation in shape) with small pale cells. Distractors: schistocytes (intravascular hemolysis), spherocytes (hereditary spherocytosis), sickle cells, dacrocytes (marrow fibrosis).

Trap

Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant. Inflammation can push it into the normal range even with low stores.

Q5. In what order does iron run out?
1 Storage (ferritin) empties → 2 Circulating iron (transferrin) falls → 3 Cells get smaller, then fewer → anemia

Anemia appears only after stores are depleted. Treatment refills in reverse: circulating iron first (Hb normalizes), stores last.

Q6. How is it treated?
  • Oral or parenteral iron; transfusion if severe
  • Marrow response (↑ retics) in about 10 days
  • Find and treat the cause; rule out malignancy in adults
  • Follow up; infection, inflammation or cancer can blunt the response
Trap

A normal Hb does not mean treatment is finished. Keep giving iron until the storage compartment (ferritin) is refilled.

ApplyLO 6 application questions

A1A 27-year-old woman has fatigue and a new craving to chew ice. Best next step?
Order serum ferritin. Pagophagia points to iron deficiency; confirm with iron studies in an adult. Not electrophoresis, GI cancer screening or marrow biopsy first.
A2A 69-year-old man has Hb 9.3 and MCV 67 with no obvious bleeding. After confirming iron deficiency, what must be done?
Endoscopy/colonoscopy to find occult GI blood loss; GI cancer is found in 8–10% of men and postmenopausal women.
A3A patient on long-term omeprazole develops a microcytic anemia. Mechanism?
Higher gastric pH → less conversion to absorbable Fe²⁺ → impaired absorption.

LO 7Thalassemias

Q1. What is a thalassemia, and how is it different from sickle cell?
  • Inherited quantitative disorders of hemoglobin: decreased or absent synthesis of α or β globin chains
  • Result: less normal Hb + shortened RBC survival
  • Sickle cell is qualitative: a normal amount of an abnormal globin
  • Common in Mediterranean, African and Southeast Asian populations
α-thalassemiaβ-thalassemia
Gene2 α genes on chromosome 16 (4 total)1 β gene on chromosome 11 (2 total)
Usual defectGene deletionsPoint mutations (>100) affecting RNA synthesis, processing or stability
Excess chainβ (or γ in the fetus)α
Memory tool

Alpha: 4 genes on chromosome 16 (4 × 4 = 16). Beta: 2 genes on chromosome 11. Alpha is usually a deletion; beta is usually a point mutation.

Q2. Why does missing one chain make red cells die?
  • Iron overload: the body senses too few RBCs and absorbs more iron (plus transfusions), though iron is not the problem
  • Extramedullary hematopoiesis in spleen and liver; more common in β
Q3. How do α-thalassemias scale with the number of deleted genes?
Genes deletedConditionResult
1Silent carrierNo anemia
2α-thalassemia traitMild microcytic anemia
3HbH disease (β₄)Moderate to severe anemia
4Hydrops fetalis (Hb Barts, γ₄)Incompatible with life
Step 1

α-trait cis deletion (both on one chromosome; Asian ancestry) → offspring at risk of hydrops; trans deletion (African ancestry) → less risk. Two-gene deletion has a normal electrophoresis.

Q4. What does β-thalassemia major look like?

Two abnormal β genes (homozygous). Appears in infancy / early childhood as HbF falls: poor feeding, failure to thrive, pallor.

Labs and smear

  • Hb very low: 2.5–6 g/dL
  • ↓ MCV; microcytic, hypochromic
  • Target cells, nucleated RBCs
  • Retics ↑ but lower than expected
  • ↑ HbF (and HbA2)
  • Serum iron normal to high
  • Diagnosis: Hb electrophoresis + quantitation of HbA, HbA2, HbF

From severe anemia → ↑ EPO

  • Erythroid hyperplasia in many bones → skull and facial deformities ("chipmunk" face, "crew-cut" skull X-ray)
  • Impaired bone growth
  • Hepatosplenomegaly (sequestration + extramedullary hematopoiesis)
  • Cachexia

Facial bone expansion

  • Marrow expands into maxilla and skull

Hepatosplenomegaly

  • Big liver and spleen, poor growth

Spleen · face · skull

  • "Hair-on-end" skull from marrow expansion

Iron overload

  • Brown hemosiderin in liver → secondary hemochromatosis

Iron overload and treatment

  • Iron from increased absorption + transfusions → secondary hemochromatosis: heart, liver, pancreas, joints
  • Untreated: fatal. Treat with transfusions + iron chelation; bone marrow transplant can cure
  • Leading cause of death: congestive heart failure (cardiac iron)
In-house exam

Classic stem: a young child of Mediterranean (Italian/Sicilian) descent, poor appetite, underweight, hepatosplenomegaly, Hb 6, hypochromic microcytic smear, normal serum iron, retics 10%, marrow with erythroid hyperplasia and ↑ iron. Answer: imbalance of α and β globin chains.

Step 1

An adolescent with lifelong anemia, delayed puberty, hyperglycemia, ↑ TSH and ferritin ≈700 = β-thalassemia with transfusion iron overload hitting pancreas, pituitary and thyroid. Chelators: deferoxamine, deferasirox.

Q5. How is β-thalassemia minor different from iron deficiency?

One abnormal gene (trait). More common; mild, often asymptomatic microcytic anemia; mild erythroid hyperplasia. The point is to not mistake it for iron deficiency: treatment differs and genetic counseling matters.

ApplyLO 7 application questions

A1A 10-month-old is fussy and eating poorly; Hb 6, hepatosplenomegaly, target cells, normal serum iron, retics 10%. Cause?
Imbalance of α and β globin chains (β-thalassemia major). Symptoms start as HbF wanes after ~6 months.
A2A healthy 24-year-old Greek man has Hb 12.1, MCV 63, RBC 5.9 million, normal RDW and ferritin. Next test and what to avoid?

A3A transfusion-dependent 19-year-old with β-thal major develops dyspnea and edema. Most likely cause of death in this disease?
Congestive heart failure from cardiac iron overload (secondary hemochromatosis).

LO 8Sickle cell anemia

Q1. What is the genetic defect, and who is affected?
  • Point mutation in codon 6 of the β-globin gene (A → T) → valine replaces glutamic acid → HbS
  • Autosomal recessive; a qualitative hemoglobinopathy
  • More common with ancestry from sub-Saharan Africa, Cuba, South/Central America, Saudi Arabia, India, Turkey, Greece, Italy
  • US: ≈72,000 with SS disease; 1 in 500 African American births; 1 in 1,000–1,400 Hispanic American births; ≈2 million with trait
Sickle cell trait (HbAS)Sickle cell disease (HbSS)
HbS≈40% of Hb≈100%
SymptomsUsually noneHemolysis, vaso-occlusion, organ damage
MCVNormalUsually normocytic; Hb 5–9 g/dL
Step 1

Trait protects against falciparum malaria (heterozygote advantage). Trait can still cause renal medullary carcinoma and microscopic hematuria/isosthenuria.

Q2. What makes a red cell sickle?
  • On deoxygenation, HbS molecules aggregate and polymerize → long, narrow cells; viscous, sticky cytoplasm
  • Reversible at first; repeated cycles → irreversibly sickled cells with membrane damage
  • Factors that increase sickling:
    • Amount of HbS (SS ≫ trait)
    • Intracellular dehydration (↑ HbS concentration)
    • Longer exposure to low O₂ tension
Step 1

Sickling triggers: hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration, high altitude, infection, cold.

Q3. What are the three arms of sickle cell pathophysiology?
In-house exam

A child with sickle cell, abdominal and hip pain: the mechanism is ischemia. Infection and inflammation trigger sickling; adhesion molecules make sickled cells stick and occlude vessels → micro-thrombi → infarction.

Step 1

  • Salmonella osteomyelitis; dactylitis (painful hand/foot swelling) in infants; renal papillary necrosis.
  • Parvovirus B19 → aplastic crisis.
Q5. How is it diagnosed?
  • Clinical suspicion → CBC and indices → peripheral smear (sickle cells, target cells)
  • Hemoglobin electrophoresis confirms: HbS has a distinct migration pattern

ApplyLO 8 application questions

A1An 11-year-old has fever, severe leg pain and fatigue after a recent chest cold, with similar past episodes. Diagnosis?
Sickle cell disease (vaso-occlusive crisis triggered by infection).
A2A 12-year-old with sickle cell has Howell-Jolly bodies on smear. What happened to his spleen, and which organisms threaten him?

A3A sickle cell patient develops fever, cough, chest pain and hypoxia with a new infiltrate. What is it?
Acute chest syndrome: sickled cells occlude pulmonary vessels; a medical emergency.

1A 27-year-old woman has increasing fatigue and a new craving to chew ice chips. What is the most appropriate next step?

Pagophagia suggests iron deficiency; in an adult, confirm with iron studies (ferritin). A premenopausal woman's most likely cause is menstrual loss.

2An 11-year-old boy has fever, severe leg pain and fatigue a few weeks after a chest cold. He has had similar pain before, which he blamed on soccer. Most likely diagnosis?

Recurrent pain episodes triggered by infection = vaso-occlusive crises of sickle cell disease.

3A 10-month-old boy feeds poorly, is at the 10th percentile and has hepatosplenomegaly. Hb 6 g/dL, normal serum iron, retics 10%; marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron. Most likely cause?

β-thalassemia major. Unpaired α chains precipitate → ineffective erythropoiesis; ↑ iron absorption; ↑ HbF.

4A 10-year-old girl with sickle cell anemia and several past pneumonias has severe left upper quadrant and left hip pain. Hb 7 g/dL. The pain is most likely caused by:

Ischemia from vaso-occlusion: sickled cells adhere and occlude vessels → infarction (spleen, femoral head).

5A 42-year-old woman with fibroids and heavy periods has Hb 9.5, MCV 68, ferritin 5, transferrin saturation 10%, TIBC 500. Most likely peripheral smear finding?

Iron deficiency from chronic blood loss: hypochromic, microcytic cells with marked variation in shape (poikilocytosis) and size.

6A 12-year-old with lifelong pain crises has Hb 11.2 and electrophoresis showing 93% HbS, 6% HbF, 1% HbA2. Hydroxyurea helps him. Its benefit comes mainly from an increase in:

Hydroxyurea raises HbF, which does not polymerize with HbS.

7A 17-year-old girl with lifelong fatigue has not entered puberty; she has hepatosplenomegaly, Hb 9.1, MCV 66, target cells, glucose 144, TSH 6.2 and ferritin 679. A mutation in which gene is most likely?

β-thalassemia with iron overload (absorption + transfusions) injuring pancreas, pituitary and thyroid. HFE causes primary hemochromatosis without a lifelong microcytic anemia.

8Two patients have MCV 66. Which pair of findings best supports thalassemia trait over iron deficiency?

Thal trait: uniform small cells (normal RDW) and a normal-to-high RBC count (Mentzer 66 ÷ 6 = 11 <13). The others describe iron deficiency.

9A 70-year-old man has new iron deficiency anemia and no symptoms other than fatigue. Which is the most important next step?

In men and postmenopausal women, iron deficiency means occult GI blood loss until proven otherwise (GI cancer in 8–10%).

10Why is a child with β-thalassemia major well at birth?

Newborns run on HbF; β-chain disorders appear around 6 months as γ is replaced by β.

11A 7-year-old with sickle cell disease is admitted with pneumococcal sepsis. Which change most explains his risk?

Autosplenectomy → loss of splenic clearance of encapsulated organisms.

12Which iron study pattern fits iron deficiency?

Iron deficiency: ↓ iron, ↑ TIBC, ↓ ferritin. A = chronic disease; B = hemochromatosis; D = thal trait.
