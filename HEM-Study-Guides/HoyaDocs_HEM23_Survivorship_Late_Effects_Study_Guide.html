LO 1The mission of long-term follow-up

Q1. Why is "cure not enough"?
NumberMeaning
≈85%Children with cancer who survive >5 years (most cured)
>500,000U.S. childhood cancer survivors; ≈1 in 530 adults aged 20–39; >100,000 in college
<5%Survivors actually followed in a survivorship program
15–30 yearsWhen most late effects appear after therapy ends
75% / >40%Treated 1970s–1990s: 75% develop a chronic disease by 40; >40% a serious problem
  • Late effects come from chemo, radiation, surgery, stem cell transplant and immunotherapy; some appear during treatment, most years later
  • Excess early death, in order: (1) second malignancy, (2) cardiovascular, (3) pulmonary disease
  • Risk of serious treatment-related disease is still significantly elevated beyond 30 years from diagnosis
In-house exam

When is the risk of serious treatment-related disease highest? It remains significantly elevated beyond 30 years after diagnosis: it doesn't fade.

Analogy — the time bomb

The patient feels well and leaves pediatrics. In their 30s the heart, breast or second-cancer problem goes off, and no one remembers the treatment. Survivorship programs cover that gap.

Q2. What is the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study?
  • Largest NIH-funded follow-up study: 17,308 survivors treated 1970–1986 at 26 centers, <21 at diagnosis, survived >5 y
  • Compared 10,397 adult survivors with 3,034 siblings → ≈75% had ≥1 chronic condition 30 years after diagnosis
  • Tracked growth, organ function, fertility, second cancers, psychosocial issues and early mortality
Q3. What does a survivorship program set out to do?
  1. Follow survivors carefully for anticipated late effects
  2. Educate them about their cancer and its treatment
  3. Educate on risk-taking behaviors, healthy lifestyle, fertility, employment, health insurance
  4. Transition them to adult programs as they age
  • Eligibility: all patients 2 years off therapy
  • History: 1960s–mid-1980s, survivors were discharged 5–10 years off therapy and never seen again
  • National Academy of Medicine: lifelong, risk-based survivorship care
Memory tool — F-E-E-T

Follow · Educate (cancer) · Educate (life) · Transition. Ticket in = 2 years off therapy.

ApplyLO 1 application question

A1A child finished leukemia therapy 2 years ago and is doing well. Should she enter a long-term follow-up program?
Yes: all patients 2 years off therapy qualify.

LO 2The most common late effects

Q1. Which late effects do survivors ask about, and which one kills them?

What survivors ask about

  • Neurocognitive dysfunction
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Infertility and gonadal dysfunction
  • Psychosocial problems

What they never ask about

  • Second cancer, the leading cause of excess late death
  • The physician has to raise it
Q2. What are the neurocognitive and growth effects?

Neurocognitive

  • Most at risk: brain tumors and ALL; also after SCT or head/neck radiation
  • Cause: cranial radiation, CNS-directed chemo
  • Mild deficits → major learning disabilities: reading, spelling, math; attention, memory; processing speed; visual-motor
  • Special education needed in 8–50%

Growth

  • Cranial radiation → early puberty (early growth-plate fusion)
  • Spinal radiation >35 Gy → direct inhibition of vertebral growth → short trunk
  • Chemo-related growth slowing is usually temporary

Cranial radiation and growth

  • 24 Gy at age 2 → menarche at 10, predicted adult height 147 cm
Step 1

Cranial radiation also causes GH deficiency (most radiation-sensitive pituitary axis); high doses can cause delayed puberty. Check growth velocity and test GH.

Q3. How does treatment damage the heart?
  • Anthracyclines (doxorubicin, daunorubicin) → asymptomatic cardiotoxicity → cardiomyopathy, LV dysfunction, CHF
  • Mantle (chest) radiation → coronary and carotid artery disease, valve disease
  • Risk factors: female sex, younger age at treatment, higher cumulative dose, radiation to the heart
  • Cardiovascular disease = 2nd cause of excess late death
Memory tool — YF-BDR

Younger · Female · Big cumulative Dose · Radiation to the heart.

Step 1

Anthracycline = dose-dependent, irreversible dilated cardiomyopathy (free radicals, iron). Dexrazoxane (iron chelator) protects; monitor with echo. Trastuzumab = reversible, not dose-dependent.

Q4. How does treatment affect fertility in boys vs girls?
MalesFemales
SensitivityTestes very sensitiveOvaries relatively resistant to chemo, sensitive to radiation
Damaging exposure≈10 g cyclophosphamide; low-dose radiation 200–300 cGyAbdominal/pelvic radiation (22–30 Gy), high-dose alkylators, cranial RT, TBI
ResultSterility (azoospermia); testosterone usually keptPubertal delay, premature ovarian failure → osteoporosis, early CAD
Age—Older girls (13–19, fewer follicles) at higher risk
PreventSperm bankingOocyte / ovarian tissue freezing
  • TBI before BMT = highest risk: premature ovarian failure in 100% of girls >10 y, 50% <10 y
  • Why osteoporosis and early CAD? Loss of estrogen, which protects bone and vessels
Memory tool

"Testes break easily; ovaries fear radiation."

In-house exam

Know the numbers: 10 g cyclophosphamide or 200–300 cGy sterilizes boys; TBI is the top risk for ovarian failure.

Therapy-related MDS/AML has a worse prognosis than primary disease. Pancytopenia, ringed sideroblasts and del(5q) 7 years after lymphoma chemo → t-MDS.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A 28-year-old who received doxorubicin at age 8 now has dyspnea, an S3 and an ejection fraction of 30%. Cause?
Anthracycline cardiomyopathy (dilated, dose-dependent).
A2A 17-year-old who had 40 Gy of spinal radiation for medulloblastoma is much shorter than his family, with a short trunk. Why?
Radiation directly inhibited vertebral growth (>35 Gy).
A3A 30-year-old treated with leg radiation for Ewing sarcoma develops a new mass in the same field. Most likely?
Radiation-induced sarcoma (in-field second cancer).

LO 3The emotional burden of survivorship

Q1. What psychological problems do survivors face?
  • More likely to have mental health disorders (anxiety, depression), chronic pain, fatigue, sleep disorders
  • PTSD in ≈1 in 5; can emerge months to years after treatment
  • Parents get PTSD too
  • Social burden: employment, health insurance, fertility worries, risk-taking
In-house exam

PTSD affects about one fifth of survivors (and their parents) and may appear years after treatment.

Step 1

PTSD: intrusion, avoidance, negative mood, hyperarousal for >1 month. Treat with trauma-focused CBT ± SSRI.

Q2. Why do survivors drop out of follow-up?
BarrierStrategy to bring them back
Relied on parents to track appointmentsEducation near the end of treatment for survivors and parents
Too young to know treatment detailsTreatment summary; Passport for Care
Want normalcy, "done with cancer"Theme: focus on wellness
Lost after high schoolRemote yearly check-ups via telehealth
Not interested in lifestyle educationSupport groups; survivorship conferences on specific late effects
Adult doctors unfamiliar with late effectsSimplified COG guidelines; educate primary care providers
Trap

A survivor with nightmares who misses visits isn't just "noncompliant": avoidance is a PTSD symptom. Respond with empathy and a psychology referral.

ApplyLO 3 application question

A1A 19-year-old says: "I'm done with cancer. I'm not going back to the children's hospital." Best response?
Acknowledge his wish for normalcy, frame follow-up around wellness and his goals, and offer telehealth or transition to adult/PCP care.

LO 4The survivorship team and communication

Q1. Who is on the team, and what are the core components of care?

Team

  • MD
  • Nurse practitioner
  • Social worker
  • Psychologist
  • Nutritionist
  • + the primary care physician as an integral partner

Core components

  1. Cancer treatment summary and plan (drugs, cumulative doses, radiation fields)
  2. COG Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines
  3. Delivery of risk-based care

Shared care model

  • Pediatrician ↔ oncologist ↔ PCP, joined by communication
Memory tool

Team = "My Nurse Says Please Nourish": MD · NP · Social worker · Psychologist · Nutritionist.

Q2. How does transition to adult care work?
  • Move from the sheltered pediatric environment to the independent adult one
  • Challenge: an effective handoff between pediatric- and adult-centered providers
  • Optimal care: longitudinal, survivor-focused care into young adulthood so screening and psychosocial support continue
  • Models: pediatric oncology center, adult oncology center, primary care, formal transition programs (e.g., STAR), or a combination
Trap — communication questions

The best answer is rarely "insist they come" or "talk to the parents instead." Choose the patient-centered option: explore concerns, frame follow-up around wellness, give anticipatory guidance, speak to the survivor directly (an adult at 18), and partner with the PCP.

High yield — lecture summary

Late effects are frequent and serious · proactive, risk-based care reduces morbidity · the PCP is an integral partner · education of survivors, families and PCPs is essential.

ApplyLO 4 application question

A1A 20-year-old college student had Wilms tumor at 6, doesn't know his treatment and hasn't seen an oncologist in 8 years. First step?
Get his treatment summary (Passport for Care) and apply COG guidelines for risk-based care with his PCP.
