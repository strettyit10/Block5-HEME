LO 1Normal platelets and primary hemostasis

Q1. What are platelets, and where do they come from?
  • Smallest circulating blood cells: 1–4 µm
  • Anucleate (can't make new proteins) but very active
  • Lifespan 7–10 days; ≈⅓ sit in the spleen at any time
  • Made by megakaryocytes in marrow; adults make 35,000–50,000/µL per day, up to 8× more on demand
  • Thrombopoietin (from kidney and liver) drives megakaryocyte production and differentiation
  • Main job: stop bleeding after vessel injury. With intact vessels, most platelets never touch the endothelium

Platelets on smear

  • Small purple fragments between RBCs

Megakaryocyte

  • Huge, lobulated nucleus; sheds platelets
Q2. Which platelet receptors bind which ligands?
Ligand (in subendothelium/plasma)Platelet receptorStep
von Willebrand factorGPIb/IX (also GPIIb/IIIa once activated)Tethering / adhesion
FibrinogenGPIIb/IIIa (integrin αIIbβ3)Aggregation (platelet–platelet bridge)
CollagenListed as GPIIb/IIIa in lectureAdhesion + activation

Subendothelial matrix: collagen (most potent: activates platelets and binds vWF), vWF, laminin, fibronectin, thrombospondin.

Trap

GPIb is the only receptor on a resting (non-activated) platelet with real affinity for activated vWF. GPIIb/IIIa only works after activation.

Step 1

Collagen's main direct platelet receptors are GPVI and GPIa/IIa; vWF bridges collagen to GPIb. Remember "Ib = binds vWF; IIb/IIIa = bridges fibrinogen."

Q3. What are the steps of primary hemostasis?
  • Activation is a controlled release reaction: granules fuse with the membrane and dump contents → recruits and activates more platelets (amplification)
  • The space between aggregating platelets traps ADP and thromboxane A2 → more activation → "inside-out" signaling → irreversible aggregates (fibrinogen, vWF, fibronectin)
Memory tool

"The Red Army Always Fights": Tether, Roll, Activate, Aggregate, Firm adhesion.

Analogy — the velcro and the zip ties

GPIb + vWF is the velcro that catches a passing platelet on the damaged wall. GPIIb/IIIa + fibrinogen are the zip ties that bind platelets to each other.

LO 2Low and high platelet counts

Q1. How is thrombocytopenia defined and graded?
GradePlatelet count (/µL)
Normal≥150,000 (to ≈450,000)
Mild100,000–150,000
Moderate50,000–99,000
Severe<50,000

Bleeding risk does not track neatly with the count: it varies with the underlying condition and can be very unpredictable.

Q2. What are the four mechanisms of a low count?
MechanismExamples and clues
↓ Production in marrowUsually other lines are low too: MDS, infection, B12/folate deficiency; rarely isolated megakaryocyte loss from a drug; alcohol (direct marrow toxicity)
Sequestration in spleenNormal spleen holds ⅓; a big spleen can hold up to 90% → liver disease/cirrhosis, myelofibrosis
Antibody-mediated destructionAntiplatelet antibodies → removal by monocyte/macrophage system: drug-induced, autoimmune disease, primary ITP
ConsumptionThrombotic microangiopathy (TTP, HUS), DIC

Splenomegaly

  • Big spleen pools platelets → low count, normal marrow
Memory tool

Low platelets = "Made, Moved, Murdered, or Munched": ↓ production, sequestered, antibody-destroyed, consumed.

Q3. What do I need to know about drug-induced thrombocytopenia?
  • Almost any drug can do it; mechanism: drug-dependent platelet-reactive antibody → splenic removal
  • Starts 1–2 weeks after daily exposure to a new drug; within hours on re-exposure
  • Resolves 5–7 days after stopping; specific therapy rarely needed
  • Over-the-counter triggers: quinine-containing beverages (tonic water), herbal preparations, recent vaccinations, acetaminophen, nuts; alcohol (direct marrow toxicity)
Step 1 — heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

HIT type II: 5–10 days after starting heparin, IgG against heparin–PF4 complexes activates platelets → platelets fall >50% and thrombosis (paradoxical clots). Stop all heparin; use a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban) or fondaparinux.

Q4. What about a high count (thrombocytosis)?

Thrombocytosis = platelet count above the upper limit of normal (≈450,000/µL).

TypeCauses
Reactive (most common)Iron deficiency, inflammation/infection, bleeding, malignancy, after splenectomy
ClonalEssential thrombocythemia and other myeloproliferative neoplasms (JAK2, CALR, MPL)
Trap

Mild thrombocytosis with a microcytic anemia is usually iron deficiency, not a neoplasm.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A man with cirrhosis and a big spleen has platelets of 60,000 and a normal marrow. Mechanism?
Splenic sequestration (and ↓ TPO from the liver).
A2A patient who drinks tonic water nightly develops sudden petechiae and platelets of 8,000. Likely cause?
Quinine-induced (drug-dependent antibody) thrombocytopenia; resolves after stopping.

Q2. How does platelet aggregometry work?
  1. Make platelet-rich plasma (cloudy)
  2. Add an agonist: ADP, arachidonic acid, collagen, epinephrine, ristocetin
  3. Aggregating platelets clump and sink → plasma clears → light transmission rises
  4. A photometer records the rate and maximum %: 0% = platelet-rich plasma, 100% = platelet-poor plasma
  5. Two waves: primary (activation) and secondary (irreversible aggregation)
Step 1

Ristocetin makes vWF bind GPIb → tests the vWF–GPIb axis. Abnormal in Bernard-Soulier and von Willebrand disease; corrected by adding normal plasma only in vWD. Bleeding time / PFA-100 is prolonged in platelet function disorders.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A patient with a platelet disorder has normal PT and PTT. Expected?
Yes: PT/PTT measure clotting factors (secondary hemostasis), not platelets.

GPIb/IX (Bernard-Soulier).
A2A teen with severe gum bleeding has a normal count; platelets aggregate only with ristocetin. Defect?
GPIIb/IIIa (Glanzmann).

LO 5Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Q1. What does ITP look like?
  • Isolated thrombocytopenia in an otherwise healthy adult: bruises, petechiae, gum/nose bleeding, heavy menses
  • Only the platelet count is low (normal WBC and Hb)
  • More common in young adult women; equal sexes in older adults. Incidence ≈3.3/100,000 adults per year
  • Associations: HIV, hepatitis C, H. pylori

Case: platelets 1,000

  • 32-year-old woman; 3 days of bruises, a non-itchy "rash" on both legs
  • No fever, meds or travel; normal WBC and Hb

Easy bruising

  • Typical platelet-type bleeding
Q2. What causes ITP?
  • Antibody-mediated peripheral destruction of platelets and/or antibody-mediated megakaryocyte destruction
  • 60–70% of adults have platelet-specific IgG against GPIIb/IIIa and/or GPIb/IX/V
  • Antibody-coated platelets are removed by splenic macrophages (Fc receptors); the spleen is also a major site of antibody production
Analogy — tagged for removal

IgG is a "remove me" sticker on each platelet. Splenic macrophages read the sticker with their Fc receptors and eat the platelet. Every ITP treatment either makes fewer stickers, hides the readers, removes the spleen, or makes more platelets.

Q3. How is ITP diagnosed?
  • Clinical, presumptive diagnosis after a full history and exam rule out other causes
  • Age <60: no further testing, no bone marrow needed
  • Age >60: marrow to look at megakaryocytes (rule out MDS)
  • If done, marrow shows normal or ↑ megakaryocytes; smear may show large platelets (young platelets)
Q4. How does each treatment interfere with the disease?
TreatmentStep it blocks
Corticosteroids (first line)↓ Antibody production and ↓ macrophage clearance of coated platelets
IVIGSaturates/blocks Fc receptors on splenic macrophages → fast rise
Anti-D (Rh+ patients)Coats RBCs that compete for Fc receptors
Rituximab (anti-CD20)Depletes B cells → ↓ antibody
TPO receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag)↑ Production by megakaryocytes
SplenectomyRemoves the main site of destruction and antibody production
Platelet transfusionOnly for life-threatening bleeding; transfused platelets are coated and destroyed too
Step 1
  • Children: acute ITP 1–3 weeks after a viral infection; usually self-limited.
  • Adults: chronic ITP, women of reproductive age; maternal IgG can cross the placenta → neonatal thrombocytopenia.
  • Splenomegaly is absent in ITP; a big spleen suggests another cause.
In-house exam

Stem: heavy menses, petechiae, platelets 15,000 with enlarged platelets, no organomegaly; transfused HLA-matched platelets don't raise the count → destruction of antibody-coated platelets by the spleen.

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A 9-year-old has bruises 3 weeks after an RSV infection; platelets 11,000; other counts normal; improves with steroids. Cause?
Antiplatelet antibodies (acute ITP).
A2Why does IVIG raise the platelet count within days?
It blocks Fc receptors on splenic macrophages, so antibody-coated platelets aren't eaten.

LO 6Thrombotic microangiopathy: TTP and HUS

Q1. What is a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)?

By definition: microvascular thrombosis + thrombocytopenia + hemolytic anemia + RBC fragmentation. Endothelial injury → platelet-rich microthrombi consume platelets; RBCs passing the clots are sliced → schistocytes; the clots damage organs.

CriterionDefinition
Thrombocytopenia<150,000 or a 25% drop from baseline
Microangiopathic hemolysis↑ LDH, ↓ haptoglobin, fragmented cells, microspherocytes, schistocytes; >2 fragments per HPF (≈1% of RBCs) is significant
End-organ damageRenal, neurologic, cardiac, hepatic, GI, skin

Schistocytes

  • RBC fragments from fibrin/platelet strands

TTP smear

  • Schistocytes, very few platelets

Hereditary TMA

  • Young children
  • Complement, metabolic or coagulation defects
  • Inherited ADAMTS13 deficiency

Acquired TMA

  • Adults
  • Anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies (TTP)
  • Shiga toxin (HUS), drugs, acquired complement defects
Q2. Why does TTP happen?
  • ADAMTS13 = metalloprotease that keeps vWF multimers at normal size
  • Acquired TTP: adults, autoantibodies against ADAMTS13
  • Diagnosis: ADAMTS13 activity and antigen; activity must be <10%
  • Fatal if untreated; recognize and treat promptly
  • Treatment: plasma exchange + immunosuppression (removes antibody, replaces ADAMTS13)
High yield — the pentad (will be tested)
  1. Thrombocytopenia
  2. Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia
  3. Neurologic abnormalities
  4. Renal insufficiency
  5. Fever

The pentad actually applies to any TMA, not just TTP.

Memory tool

"FAT RN": Fever, Anemia (MAHA), Thrombocytopenia, Renal, Neuro.

Step 1

TTP: normal PT/PTT (vs DIC). Don't transfuse platelets ("fuel on the fire"). Add-on: caplacizumab (anti-vWF), rituximab.

Q3. How is HUS different?
  • Usually children
  • Triad: MAHA + acute renal insufficiency + thrombocytopenia
  • Follows infection with verotoxin (Shiga toxin)-producing bacteria: E. coli O157, Shigella, Salmonella
  • Toxin damages endothelium (especially kidney) → platelet plugs → hemolysis and organ damage
  • No direct therapy: supportive care, transfusion, dialysis

Trap

Don't give antibiotics reflexively for O157:H7 diarrhea in children; they can increase toxin release and HUS risk.

A1A 40-year-old woman has confusion, fever, platelets 12,000, schistocytes, ↑ creatinine, normal PT/PTT. Test to confirm and treatment?
ADAMTS13 activity <10%; treat with plasma exchange + immunosuppression.
A2A 4-year-old has bloody diarrhea after a petting zoo, then pallor and low urine output. Diagnosis?
HUS from Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O157.

MCQExam-style questions

Score: 0 / 0

1A 37-year-old woman has 6 months of heavy menses and new petechiae. No organomegaly. Hb 14.2, WBC normal, platelets 15,000 with enlarged platelets. HLA-matched platelet transfusion does not raise her count. Basis of her bleeding?

Chronic ITP: IgG against GPIIb/IIIa or GPIb/IX coats both native and transfused platelets; spleen removes them.

2A 9-year-old has bruises 3 weeks after RSV pneumonia. Platelets 11,300; other counts normal. He improves with corticosteroids. Cause?

Acute ITP after a viral infection in a child.

3A 21-year-old with protein C deficiency starts anticoagulation for PE. Two weeks later she has a left MCA stroke; platelets 65,400; PTT 51. Which drug caused this?

HIT type II: IgG to heparin–PF4 activates platelets → thrombocytopenia with paradoxical thrombosis.

4A child with recurrent nosebleeds has platelets 85,000 with giant platelets. Aggregation is normal with ADP, collagen and epinephrine but absent with ristocetin. Deficient protein?

Bernard-Soulier: GPIb/IX defect, giant platelets, ristocetin-only failure.

5A teenager has severe gum bleeding and a normal platelet count and size. Platelets fail to aggregate to ADP, collagen and epinephrine but aggregate with ristocetin. Diagnosis?

Ristocetin (vWF–GPIb) works; everything needing GPIIb/IIIa fails → Glanzmann.

6Which receptor on a resting platelet captures activated vWF at a site of injury?

GPIb is the only resting-platelet receptor with significant affinity for activated vWF.

7A 35-year-old woman has fever, confusion, platelets 14,000, schistocytes, ↑ LDH, ↑ creatinine, and normal PT/PTT. What is the best next treatment?

TTP (pentad, ADAMTS13 <10%): fatal untreated; plasma exchange + immunosuppression.

8In TTP, the deficient ADAMTS13 normally does what?

ADAMTS13 is the metalloprotease that keeps vWF multimers small; without it, platelets get trapped.

9A 5-year-old develops pallor, low platelets and acute kidney injury a week after bloody diarrhea. Most likely trigger?

HUS: MAHA + renal failure + thrombocytopenia after verotoxin-producing bacteria.

10Which ITP treatment works mainly by blocking Fc receptors on splenic macrophages?

IVIG saturates Fc receptors; rituximab depletes B cells; romiplostim ↑ production.

11A 30-year-old has isolated thrombocytopenia (20,000), no symptoms other than petechiae, normal exam and normal other counts. What is required for the diagnosis of ITP?

ITP is a clinical/presumptive diagnosis; under age 60 no marrow is needed.

12Which granule contents are released to amplify aggregation?

Dense granules (Ca²⁺, ADP, serotonin, histamine) amplify the platelet response.
