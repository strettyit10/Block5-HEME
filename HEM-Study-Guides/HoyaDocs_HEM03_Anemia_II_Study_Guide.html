LO 1Macrocytic anemia

Q1. What labs define a macrocytic anemia?
  • Low Hb/Hct with MCV >100 fL (megaloblastic often 100–150 fL)
  • RDW ↑ in megaloblastic anemia
  • Reticulocyte count splits the list: high → hemolysis or bleeding (big young cells); normal/low → B12/folate, marrow, liver, thyroid, alcohol, drugs
  • Peripheral smear: look for oval macrocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils (megaloblastic)
Q2. What makes a macrocytic anemia "megaloblastic"?

Megaloblastic anemias are the macrocytic anemias in which the marrow shows distinctive abnormal red cell precursors. The megaloblast is the hallmark: a product of impaired DNA synthesis, almost always from B12 (cobalamin) or folate deficiency.

Normoblast vs megaloblast

  • Megaloblast is bigger, with an immature, open nucleus in a maturing cytoplasm

Megaloblastic marrow

  • Hypercellular; large precursors in all lines
Analogy — the slow printer

DNA is the printer, cytoplasm is the paper tray. B12/folate lack slows the printer, but the tray keeps filling with hemoglobin. The cell waits to divide, grows large, and the nucleus looks "younger" than the cytoplasm: nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony.

Trap

Not every macrocytosis is megaloblastic. Alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism and reticulocytosis give round macrocytes without hypersegmented neutrophils.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1MCV 108, retics 0.4%, hypersegmented neutrophils. Which mechanism and which two vitamins?
Impaired DNA synthesis (megaloblastic, production problem): B12 or folate deficiency.
A2MCV 104 with retics 11% and a rising bilirubin. Megaloblastic?
Probably not: large reticulocytes from hemolysis raise the MCV.

LO 2B12 and folate deficiency

Q1. What do B12 and folate actually do?

B12 is also needed for folate to enter and stay inside cells, which is why B12 deficiency causes a folate-type DNA block.

High yield

Homocysteine ↑ in both; MMA ↑ only in B12. That one difference separates the two deficiencies on labs.

In-house exam

Classic stem: terminal ileum resected for Crohn disease → fatigue, MCV 118, retics 0.3% → smear shows oval macrocytes. Distractors: target cells (hemoglobinopathies), sickle cells, basophilic stippling (lead), bite cells (G6PD).

Step 1

Other B12 causes: metformin, long-term PPIs/H2 blockers, nitrous oxide, bacterial overgrowth (blind loop), pancreatic insufficiency (can't free B12 from R-binder).

Step 1

Folate antagonists: methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin (also pyrimethamine). Folate deficiency in pregnancy → neural tube defects.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A man who drinks heavily and eats little develops macrocytic anemia in 4 months with normal MMA. Which vitamin?
Folate: stores last only 3–5 months; MMA is normal in folate deficiency.
A2A Scandinavian man who eats raw fish has B12 deficiency. Cause?
Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm) competing for B12.

LO 3Pernicious anemia

Q1. What is pernicious anemia?
  • Autoimmune destruction of the gastric mucosa with an inherited tendency; all ethnic groups; usually adults 50–70
  • Antibodies:
    • Anti-parietal cell → chronic atrophic gastritis → ↓ intrinsic factor and achlorhydria
    • Two anti-IF antibodies: one blocks B12 binding to IF; one blocks the B12–IF complex binding the ileal receptor
  • Result: B12 can't be absorbed → megaloblastic anemia

Atrophic gastritis

  • Top: thin, smooth atrophic mucosa
  • Bottom: normal folds
Step 1

Type A (autoimmune) gastritis affects the body/fundus; associated with other autoimmune diseases (Hashimoto, type 1 diabetes, vitiligo). ↑ gastrin from achlorhydria → gastric carcinoid risk.

Q2. What findings come with pernicious anemia beyond the anemia?
SystemFindings
BloodMegaloblastic anemia (corrected by B12); leukopenia with hypersegmented neutrophils; mild jaundice → lemon-yellow skin (mild hemolysis + pallor)
GIAtrophic glossitis (smooth red tongue); chronic atrophic gastritis + intestinal metaplasia → ↑ gastric carcinoma risk
CNSSubacute combined degeneration: myelin loss in dorsal and lateral spinal tracts → spastic paraparesis, sensory ataxia, severe paresthesias
Vascular↑ homocysteine → ↑ atherosclerosis

Glossitis

  • Smooth, red tongue from lost papillae
High yield

Neuro signs = B12, not folate. Folate deficiency can cause irritability, depression or forgetfulness, but not subacute combined degeneration.

Step 1

Dorsal columns → ↓ vibration and proprioception (+ Romberg). Lateral corticospinal tracts → spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski. Neuro damage can occur without anemia.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 62-year-old with vitiligo has MCV 116, a smooth red tongue and lost vibration sense in her feet. Which antibodies?
Anti-intrinsic factor and anti-parietal cell antibodies (pernicious anemia).
A2After B12 treatment, which change in pernicious anemia does not reverse?
The gastric mucosal changes (atrophic gastritis); screening for gastric cancer continues.

LO 4Diagnosis and treatment of megaloblastic anemia

Q1. What symptoms and exam findings do patients have?

Symptoms

  • Fatigue
  • Diarrhea
  • Glossitis (sore tongue)
  • Numbness, tingling

Exam

  • Pallor
  • Smooth tongue
  • Weight loss
  • Abnormal neurologic exam
Q2. How is the diagnosis made, step by step?
  1. Establish macrocytic anemia: ↓ Hct, MCV 100–150, ↑ RDW
  2. Establish megaloblastic: smear (oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils ≥6 lobes) ± marrow
  3. B12, folate or both? Serum B12, serum and RBC folate, MMA and homocysteine
  4. Find the cause: anti-IF / anti-parietal cell antibodies (pernicious anemia), history of surgery, diet, drugs

  • Shows DNA block in all lines

Q3. How is it treated?
  • Find the cause and correct it if possible
  • Parenteral B12 (IM or deep subcutaneous): cures the anemia; neurologic changes halt and sometimes reverse; gastric mucosal changes persist
  • Oral folate 1–5 mg/day until full hematologic recovery
  • Blood counts improve within weeks; patients with pernicious anemia are followed for gastric cancer
Trap

Folate alone fixes some of the anemia of B12 deficiency but lets the nerve damage progress. Until the cause is known, give parenteral B12 and oral folate together.

Step 1

After starting B12, watch for hypokalemia (rapid new cell production). Reticulocytes rise within about a week.

LO 5Hemolysis: definition and types

Q1. What is hemolytic anemia?
  • Premature removal of red cells (shortened lifespan, <120 days)
  • Marrow responds → ↑ reticulocyte count (by definition)
  • Products of hemoglobin breakdown accumulate (bilirubin)
  • Anemia appears only when destruction outpaces the marrow (up to ≈5× normal output)
  • Normal physiology: millions of old RBCs are destroyed each day by red pulp macrophages of the spleen
Q2. How is hemolysis classified?

By origin

  • Acquired
    • Immune: infection, alloantibodies (ABO, Rh), autoantibodies (warm, cold)
    • Non-immune: mechanical and other
  • Hereditary
    • Membrane: hereditary spherocytosis
    • Enzyme: G6PD deficiency
    • Hemoglobin: sickle cell, thalassemias

By mechanism

  • Intracorpuscular: intrinsic defect of the RBC (usually hereditary)
  • Extracorpuscular: something outside attacks a normal RBC (antibodies, mechanical, toxins)
Memory tool

Hereditary = the car is defective (membrane, engine enzyme, fuel/Hb). Acquired = a good car hit by something (antibody, fibrin strand, valve).

Q2. Which labs prove hemolysis?
  • ↓ Hematocrit; anemia often normocytic (macrocytic if many retics)
  • ↑ Reticulocyte count (marrow compensating)
  • ↑ Total and indirect bilirubin
  • ↓ Haptoglobin (especially intravascular)
  • ↑ LDH (nonspecific marker of cell turnover)
  • Smear: polychromatophilia (retics) + clue shapes: schistocytes, spherocytes, sickle cells, target cells
Memory tool — the hemolysis panel

"Up, up, up, down": ↑ retics, ↑ bilirubin (indirect), ↑ LDH, ↓ haptoglobin.

Q3. What do patients report and show?
  • Anemia: pallor, fatigue
  • Jaundice (↑ bilirubin)
  • Acute hemolysis: fever, chills, headache; back pain (Hb clogging renal tubules); dark urine
  • Chronic hemolysis: splenomegaly (working hard + extramedullary hematopoiesis), hepatomegaly, pigment gallstones
  • History: transfusions, pregnancy, drugs, infections, family history

Scleral icterus

  • Yellow sclera from unconjugated bilirubin

LO 7Immune hemolysis and antiglobulin tests

Q1. What are the immune mechanisms of hemolysis?
  1. Infection
  2. Alloantibodies: made against someone else's red cells (transfusion, pregnancy)
  3. Autoantibodies: made against the patient's own red cells (warm or cold)
Q2. What do the direct and indirect antiglobulin tests show?
Direct antiglobulin test (DAT, direct Coombs)Indirect antiglobulin test (indirect Coombs)
QuestionAre the patient's RBCs already coated with antibody or complement?Does the patient's plasma contain anti-RBC antibodies?
MethodWash patient RBCs → add anti-human globulin (anti-IgG, anti-C3d) → agglutination = positive. Then monospecific tests for IgG vs C3Patient plasma + a panel of RBCs with known antigens → add anti-human globulin → agglutination = positive
MeaningPositive = immune hemolysisPositive = alloantibodies; key before transfusion and in pregnancy
Memory tool

Direct = on the cell. Indirect = in the serum.

Q3. What happens in an ABO-incompatible transfusion?
  • Naturally occurring IgM isohemagglutinins bind the major blood group antigen → many binding sites → complement → MAC → severe intravascular hemolysis
  • ≈300/year in the US; deaths now rare; most from clerical or phlebotomy errors (half in the blood bank, half on the wards)
  • Clinical: fever, chills/rigors, diaphoresis, flushing, anxiety, vomiting, hypotension, dyspnea, tachycardia, urticaria, hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria, pain at the infusion site, abdomen, chest, back; can progress to shock

Treatment

  1. Stop the transfusion; keep venous access
  2. Check labels at the bedside; draw a patient sample
  3. Return blood, tubing and solutions to the blood bank
  4. Support the patient; anti-C5 therapy blocks the MAC
High yield

Prevention is the best therapy: careful identification. Any suspicion of a reaction → stop the transfusion first.

Q4. What is hemolytic disease of the newborn (Rh incompatibility)?
  • Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive fetus; mother sensitized by a prior pregnancy or transfusion
  • Maternal IgG alloantibodies (anti-D most common) cross the placenta and coat fetal RBCs
  • Consequences: fetal anemia, erythroid hyperplasia and extramedullary hematopoiesis, ↑ unconjugated bilirubin (jaundice after birth, CNS damage), generalized edema → hydrops fetalis
  • Prevention: RhIg to Rh-negative women at 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery, and after obstetric procedures, ectopic pregnancy, abruption, fetal demise, abortion
  • Treatment: mother → plasma exchange; baby → intrauterine transfusions

Hydrops fetalis

  • Severe anemia → heart failure → generalized edema
Step 1

Kernicterus = unconjugated bilirubin in basal ganglia. ABO HDN (mother type O with IgG anti-A/B) is usually mild and can occur in the first pregnancy; Rh HDN needs prior sensitization and is severe.

ApplyLO 7 application questions

A1Minutes into a transfusion, a patient has fever, back pain, hypotension and red urine. First action?
Stop the transfusion, keep the IV, check labels, send the unit and a sample to the blood bank.
A2An Rh-negative woman will need a transfusion. Which test checks her plasma for alloantibodies?
Indirect antiglobulin test.

IgG = "Great warm" (body temperature). IgM = "Mountain cold". Cold antibodies make fingers and ears blue (acrocyanosis).

In-house exam

Case: 30-year-old woman, fatigue for months, mild splenomegaly, retics and a rare nucleated RBC; direct and indirect Coombs positive at 37 °C but not at 4 °C → warm AIHA; look for SLE. Step 1

Cold agglutinins after Mycoplasma pneumoniae or infectious mononucleosis; chronic cold agglutinin disease with lymphoma or Waldenström. Warm AIHA with CLL, lupus, methyldopa.

Q2. How do drugs cause hemolysis?

Consider a drug in any unexplained hemolytic anemia; often hard to pin down because patients take many drugs. Three mechanisms:

MechanismExample
1. Oxidation of hemoglobinOxidant drugs in G6PD deficiency (primaquine, sulfonamides)
2. Antibody-mediatedDrug-induced immune hemolysis (penicillin, cephalosporins, methyldopa)

LO 9Hereditary spherocytosis and G6PD deficiency

Q1. What goes wrong in hereditary spherocytosis?
  • Mutations in membrane cytoskeleton proteins (spectrin, actin, ankyrin and others) that keep the cell flexible
  • Most involve ankyrin; spectrin content is reduced in many cases
  • Mixed inheritance, mostly autosomal dominant
  • Cell loses membrane → sphere: less surface area, less flexible
  • Two removal mechanisms: gets stuck in the spleen, and is recognized as foreign

Spherocytes

  • Small, round, dense; no central pallor
  • Polychromatophilic retics

Course

  • Variable: about one-third asymptomatic; others have chronic hemolysis
  • Aplastic crisis with parvovirus (infects erythroid precursors)
  • Diagnosis: clinical and family history + specific lab tests
  • Splenectomy corrects the anemia, but not the red cell defect
Step 1
  • Tests: EMA binding (flow cytometry, ↓), osmotic fragility (↑), DAT negative.
  • ↑ MCHC, ↑ RDW; splenomegaly, pigment gallstones.
  • After splenectomy: Howell-Jolly bodies, ↑ platelets.
Trap

Spherocytes appear in both HS and warm AIHA. The DAT separates them: negative in HS, positive in AIHA.

Q2. Why does G6PD deficiency cause hemolysis?
  • RBCs have no mitochondria → energy from anaerobic glycolysis
  • Glucose use: 90% Embden-Meyerhof (glycolysis → ATP, NADH); 10% hexose monophosphate shunt → NADPH
  • NADPH keeps glutathione reduced; reduced glutathione protects Hb and enzymes from oxidants (H₂O₂)
  • G6PD catalyzes the first step of the shunt
↓ G6PD → ↓ NADPH → ↓ reduced glutathione → oxidant stress → Hb denatures → Heinz bodies → membrane damage → severe: intravascular; mild: extravascular (spleen bites out Heinz bodies → bite cells)

Bite cells + Heinz bodies

  • Bite cell: part of the membrane removed
  • Inset: Heinz bodies (denatured Hb) on supravital stain
Q3. Who gets G6PD deficiency, and what triggers an attack?
  • X-linked: men fully affected; women may be heterozygous with two RBC populations
  • Hundreds of variants, most asymptomatic. Two that matter:
    • G6PD A−: ≈10% of Black Americans; milder
    • G6PD Mediterranean: Middle East; highly unstable, more severe
  • Normal at baseline; episodic intra- and extravascular hemolysis after oxidant stress:
    • Infections: viral hepatitis, pneumonia, typhoid fever
    • Drugs: antimalarials (primaquine, chloroquine), sulfonamides
    • Fava beans: 1–3 days after eating
  • Treatment: avoid triggers; most have no chronic hemolysis; transfuse for acute episodes
High yield

Fava beans + sulfa drugs + primaquine + infection in a man of African or Mediterranean descent with sudden dark urine = G6PD deficiency.

Step 1

Other triggers: dapsone, nitrofurantoin, rasburicase, isoniazid, phenacetin. Check G6PD level weeks after an episode: the oldest (most deficient) cells were destroyed, so levels can look normal during hemolysis. Protects against malaria.

ApplyLO 9 application questions

G6PD deficiency.
A2A child with hereditary spherocytosis develops sudden severe anemia with reticulocytes near zero. Cause?
Parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis.

LO 10Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and TTP

Q1. What is microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)?
  • Non-immune, mechanical hemolysis inside abnormally narrowed small vessels (fibrin strands, platelet thrombi) → RBCs sliced
  • Mostly intravascular; DAT negative
  • Smear: schistocytes (fragmented "helmet" cells)
  • Related: macroangiopathic hemolysis from prosthetic (mechanical) heart valves (turbulent shear)

Schistocytes

  • Fragments, helmet and triangle shapes
  • Normal central pallor in intact cells
Q2. Which conditions cause MAHA?
CauseMechanism / clue
TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura)Platelet-rich microthrombi; severe thrombocytopenia
DICFibrin deposition in vessels; ↑ PT/PTT, ↓ fibrinogen, ↑ D-dimer
Malignant hypertensionDamaged arterioles
Disseminated cancersTumor emboli, DIC
Drugs (thrombotic microangiopathy)See LO 8
Step 1 — TTP vs HUS
  • TTP: ↓ ADAMTS13 (autoantibody, or inherited) → large vWF multimers → platelet microthrombi. Pentad: thrombocytopenia, MAHA, fever, neuro changes, renal injury. PT/PTT normal. Treat with plasma exchange (± steroids, caplacizumab, rituximab); no platelet transfusion.
  • HUS: children after Shiga toxin E. coli O157:H7 bloody diarrhea → MAHA, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury.
  • HELLP in pregnancy.
Memory tool

TTP pentad "FAT RN": Fever, Anemia (MAHA), Thrombocytopenia, Renal, Neuro.

ApplyLO 10 application questions

A1A 35-year-old woman has confusion, fever, platelets 18,000, schistocytes, high LDH, normal PT/PTT. Best treatment?
Plasma exchange for TTP (low ADAMTS13). Avoid platelet transfusion.
A2Schistocytes with ↑ PT, ↑ PTT, ↓ fibrinogen and ↑ D-dimer in a septic patient. Cause?
DIC.

1A 36-year-old man had a terminal ileum resection for Crohn disease 2 months ago. Hb 9.6, MCV 118, retics 0.3%. Most likely smear finding?

Lymphoma + viral infection + Raynaud → IgM cold agglutinins → complement → extravascular clearance.

5Which lab result best distinguishes vitamin B12 deficiency from folate deficiency?

MMA rises only in B12 deficiency. Homocysteine, macrocytosis, hypersegmentation and low retics occur in both.

6A patient with B12 deficiency from pernicious anemia is treated with oral folate alone. Over the next months, which is most likely?

Folate bypasses part of the DNA block but does not fix B12's myelin role; neurologic damage progresses.

7Which test tells you a patient's plasma contains antibodies against red cell antigens, important before a transfusion?

Indirect Coombs = antibodies in the serum/plasma (alloantibodies). Direct = coating on the RBC.

8An ABO-incompatible unit is transfused. The hemolysis is best described as:

Naturally occurring IgM isohemagglutinins fix complement → MAC → severe intravascular hemolysis. Anti-C5 therapy can help.

9A 10-year-old with spherocytes, splenomegaly, a negative DAT and an affected father. Which protein is most often mutated?

Hereditary spherocytosis, mostly autosomal dominant; most mutations involve ankyrin.

10A woman has fever, confusion, platelets 15,000, schistocytes, ↑ LDH, ↑ creatinine and normal PT/PTT. Which deficiency explains this?

TTP: ↓ ADAMTS13 → large vWF multimers → platelet microthrombi → MAHA. Treat with plasma exchange.

11An Rh-negative woman gives birth to an Rh-positive baby. To prevent disease in her next pregnancy, she should receive RhIg:

RhIg at 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery (and after obstetric events) prevents anti-D sensitization.
