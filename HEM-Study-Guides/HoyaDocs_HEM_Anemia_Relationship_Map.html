1 · The map

Two numbers place every anemia

Rows are size (MCV). Columns are the marrow's answer (reticulocytes): low means a production problem, high means destruction or loss. Dashed chips appear in more than one box, and those crossovers are what exams like to test. Select any anemia to see its essentials and everything it connects to; connected anemias light up on the map.

Correct the retic before you trust it: corrected retic = retic % × (Hct ÷ 45); RPI = corrected ÷ ~2. RPI <2 means a production problem; >2–3 means destruction or loss. A raw 3% retic at Hct 22 is really ≈1.5% (RPI ≈0.75), an inadequate response (HEM05 Case 1).
Microcytosis cutoff: HEM02 uses MCV <70 fL; HEM06 and boards use <80 fL. Answer an in-house question with the cutoff that lecture taught.
2 · Mechanism family tree

Faucet, drain or leak

Think of RBC mass as water in a bathtub. The faucet is the marrow (production), the drain is destruction (hemolysis), and the leak is blood loss. Every anemia fits one of the three, and the thalassemias sit on two at once. Select an anemia to reveal what is missing or broken, how that becomes anemia, and its flowchart from the lecture notes. 3 · Workup path

From "anemia" to a name in four questions

The same routine runs through all four lectures: history, then MCV × corrected retics and the other cell lines, then targeted labs, then smear or marrow. Each step has the matching flowcharts from the lecture notes.

1 · Is there anemia, and what does the history point to?
↓ Hb/Hct. Then ask about menses, GI bleeding, diet/pica, meds (NSAIDs, sulfa, primaquine), surgery (bypass, ileal resection), family history, dark urine, rash/joint pain, numb feet, kidney disease.
↓
2 · How big are the cells? (MCV)
Microcytic
  • Check ferritin first
  • ↓ ferritin, ↑ TIBC → iron deficiency → adult: find the GI bleed
  • N/↑ ferritin, ↓ TIBC + inflammation → chronic disease
  • ↑ iron, ↑ ferritin, stippling → lead / sideroblastic
  • All iron normal, Mentzer <13 → Hb electrophoresis: ↑ HbA2 = β-thal minor; ↑↑ HbF = β-thal major; normal = α-thal trait possible
Normocytic
  • Check corrected retics
  • Low → creatinine (CKD), iron panel (ACD, early IDA), other cell lines low → marrow (aplastic, myelophthisic)
  • Normal MCV + high RDW → mixed deficiency (big + small cells average out)
  • High → hemolysis panel or recent bleed (go to step 3)
Macrocytic
  • Look at the smear
  • Oval macrocytes + hypersegmented PMNs → megaloblastic → B12, folate, MMA
  • ↑ MMA → B12 (anti-IF Ab → pernicious anemia); normal MMA → folate
  • Round macrocytes, no hyperseg → alcohol, liver, thyroid, MDS, drugs, or many retics
↓
3 · Is it hemolysis? ↑ LDH · ↑ indirect bilirubin · ↓ haptoglobin · ↑ retics
DAT positive: immune
  • IgG, 37 °C, spherocytes → warm AIHA
  • C3 only, 4 °C, agglutination → cold agglutinin
  • Minutes into a transfusion → ABO reaction
  • Newborn of Rh⁻ mother → HDFN
  • Penicillin, ceftriaxone, α-methyldopa → drug (antibody)
DAT negative: read the smear
  • Spherocytes, ↑ MCHC, ↑ osmotic fragility → HS
  • Bite cells, Heinz bodies → G6PD
  • Sickle cells, Howell-Jolly → sickle cell
  • Ring forms → malaria
Schistocytes: MAHA
  • ↑ PT/PTT, ↓ fibrinogen → DIC
  • Normal PT/PTT + ↓ platelets + fever, neuro, renal → TTP
  • Child after bloody diarrhea → HUS
  • Mechanical valve → valve hemolysis
Hemolysis labs with low retics and normal bilirubin is not hemolysis. It is ineffective erythropoiesis: megaloblasts (or thal precursors) dying in the marrow (HEM03 trap, HEM05 Case 2).
↓
4 · Confirm on smear or marrow, then treat
Iron to ferritin >50 · B12 lifelong and parenteral if neuro signs · folate only after B12 is excluded · treat the cause of ACD/CKD (ESA + iron in CKD) · steroids for warm AIHA · splenectomy for HS · plasma exchange for TTP · transfuse only if unstable, bleeding, ischemic, or Hb <7.

7 · Check yourself

Application questions on the relationships

Answer before you open each one.

A 38-year-old woman had a gastric bypass 9 months ago. She has numb feet, WBC 2.2, platelets 145, Hb 7.8, MCV 100, RDW 17, retics 0.5%, LDH 535, haptoglobin 15, normal bilirubin, B12 112, ferritin 20. Why is the MCV normal, and why is the LDH high?

Combined B12 + iron deficiency. Large (B12) and small (iron) cells average to a normal MCV; the high RDW and dimorphic smear give it away.

LDH ↑ and haptoglobin ↓ with low retics and normal bilirubin = ineffective erythropoiesis (megaloblasts dying in marrow), not hemolysis. Treat with parenteral B12 (neuro signs) + IV iron.

A Greek woman has been given iron twice with no improvement. MCV 64, RBC 6.0, normal RDW, ferritin 85. What test comes next, and what should happen to the iron?

Hb electrophoresis → ↑ HbA2 (β-thal minor). Mentzer 64/6.0 ≈ 10.7 (<13). Stop the iron (overload risk) and offer genetic counseling: two carriers have a 25% chance of a child with β-thal major.

A child with hereditary spherocytosis develops fever and a lacy facial rash. Hb falls from 10 to 5 and retics are 0.3%. Which other anemia in this map shares this complication, and why?

Parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis. The virus infects erythroid precursors and stops production. Any chronic hemolytic anemia (HS, sickle cell) depends on a high retic output, so a short shutdown crashes the Hb. Treat with transfusion.

A soldier with a P. vivax infection is about to start primaquine. What must be checked first, and which two inherited conditions in this map protect against falciparum malaria?

G6PD level: primaquine is an oxidant (the "oxidize" arm of drug hemolysis) and causes bite cells and Heinz bodies in G6PD deficiency. Primaquine is given for vivax/ovale hypnozoites. Sickle cell trait and G6PD deficiency both protect against P. falciparum.

A patient with a mechanical aortic valve has schistocytes and, months later, a falling ferritin and MCV. Connect the two anemias.

Valve hemolysis → iron deficiency. Chronic intravascular hemolysis spills Hb into the urine (hemoglobinuria, hemosiderinuria), so iron is lost. A destruction anemia becomes a production anemia.

A 16-year-old with β-thal major on monthly transfusions has dyspnea, edema and an irregular rhythm. Which anemia is his iron status the opposite of, and what is the treatment?

Secondary hemochromatosis (transfusions + ↑ gut absorption from ineffective erythropoiesis): the opposite of iron deficiency. Cardiac iron → CHF, the main cause of death. Treat with chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone).

A 34-year-old woman with a malar rash and arthralgias has spherocytes and retics of 9%. A 58-year-old woman with RA has MCV 79, ferritin 310, low TIBC and low retics. How does one disease lead to two different anemias?

Autoimmune disease can cause warm AIHA (IgG autoantibody, DAT +, high retics; first patient, SLE) or anemia of chronic disease (IL-6 → hepcidin → iron trapped; low retics; second patient, RA). The retic count splits them.

An 18-year-old with sickle cell disease has RUQ pain after a fatty meal. A 7-year-old with HS has an enlarged spleen and jaundice since infancy. What stone links them?

Black pigment (calcium bilirubinate) gallstones from chronic hemolysis: heme → excess unconjugated bilirubin. Distractors: cholesterol stones (↑ cholesterol:bile acid ratio) and brown stones (infection, β-glucuronidase).
