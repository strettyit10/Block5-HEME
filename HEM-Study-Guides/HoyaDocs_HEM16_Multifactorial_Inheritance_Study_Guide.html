In-house exam

Multifactorial conditions are the most common category (20–40%). Examples: oral clefts (1/500–1/1,500), NTDs (1/2,500), congenital heart defects (1/100), diabetes (1/10), schizophrenia (1/100).

Q2. How do twin studies separate genes from environment?
  • Compare concordance (both twins affected) in MZ twins (100% genes + uterus) vs DZ twins (50% genes + uterus)
EtiologyMZ concordanceDZ concordance
Purely genetic100%<100%, like ordinary siblings
Purely environmentalEqual in MZ and DZ
MultifactorialMZ > DZ, but MZ <100%
  • MZ twins reared apart separate genes from shared environment; striking concordance even for behavioral traits (alcoholism)
  • Limitations: somatic mutations after the split, epigenetic differences, random X-inactivation in females, few twins reared apart, ethical issues
High yield

Oral cleft: MZ 35–40%, DZ 4–5% → not purely genetic (MZ <100%) and not purely environmental (MZ ≠ DZ) → multifactorial.

Trap

MZ twins aren't perfect genetic copies: post-split somatic mutations, epigenetics and X-inactivation make them differ.

Q3. What makes type 2 diabetes a model adult multifactorial disease?
  • Diabetes = type 1, type 2 (80–95%), gestational; insulin secretion defects + insulin resistance
  • ≈1 in 10 Americans; many more prediabetic; higher in African American, Alaska Native/American Indian, Asian American, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander populations
  • Global epidemic → environment matters: BMI, gestational diabetes, diet, BP, stress, lipids, inactivity
  • >150 loci; each adds a small risk, and risk adds up; not every carrier gets diabetes. Genes: TCF7L2, ABCC8, CAPN10, GLUT2, GCGR, PTPN1
Family historyLifetime DM2 risk
One parent affected40%
Both parents affected70%
First-degree relative3× the population risk
TwinsMZ ≈70%, DZ 20–30%
In-house exam

Both parents with type 2 diabetes → ≈70% lifetime risk; lifestyle modification (exercise, weight loss) is the key intervention.

Analogy — filling a bucket

Each risk allele and each environmental exposure adds a cup of water to a bucket. Disease appears only when the bucket overflows (the threshold). Relatives start with a fuller bucket.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A trait has 100% MZ and 50% DZ concordance. Genetic, environmental or multifactorial?
Purely genetic (single-gene pattern).
A2A disorder has MZ and DZ concordance of 20% each. Interpretation?
Environmental: equal concordance despite different genetic sharing.

LO 2Neural tube defects and oral clefts

Q1. What is a neural tube defect, and what types exist?
  • Failure of the neural tube to develop or close → brain and spine defects
  • The neural tube forms 18–30 days after conception (before most women know they're pregnant)
  • ≈90% isolated; causes = genetics + nutrition + environment; managed by a team (neurosurgery, orthopedics, urology, nursing)
TypeDefectOutcome
AnencephalyBrain fails to develop (cranial end)Fatal
EncephaloceleBrain herniates through a skull defectRare, often fatal; ID and neuro deficits; consider a syndrome
Spina bifida occultaVertebral arch defect onlyMild (tuft of hair, dimple)
MeningoceleMeninges herniateMilder
MyelomeningoceleMeninges + cord/nerves herniateParalysis, bladder/bowel, leg deformities

Anterior vs posterior defects

  • Anterior → anencephaly; posterior → spina bifida

Spina bifida spectrum

  • Occulta → meningocele → myelomeningocele

Neural folds fusing

  • Ridges fold up and fuse into the tube
Step 1

Open NTDs → ↑ maternal serum AFP and ↑ amniotic AChE. Myelomeningocele often comes with Chiari II and hydrocephalus. Anencephaly → polyhydramnios (no swallowing).

Q2. What causes NTDs, and how are they prevented?

Genetic

  • MTHFR 677C>T (best studied): ↓ folate processing → ↑ homocysteine; mothers of affected infants often homozygous
  • GRHL3, CELSR1 (planar cell polarity), VANGL2 (neurulation)
  • Homozygous MTHFR in adults: hearing loss, heart disease, stroke

Environmental

  • Maternal obesity, poorly controlled diabetes
  • Drugs: phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate; antifolate methotrexate
  • Excess alcohol; hyperthermia
  • Low folic acid diet (the "home run")
WhoFolic acid
All women of reproductive age0.4–0.8 mg/day (prevents up to 70% of NTDs; half of pregnancies are unplanned)
Prior affected pregnancy4 mg/day from ≥1 month before conception through the 1st trimester
  • US grain fortification (1998) → 27–35% drop in NTDs; incidence now ≈1/2,500 (was 1/1,000); highest among Hispanic women in the US
  • Prenatal: screen with fetal markers; diagnose by ultrasound or amniocentesis

Fortification works

  • Prevalence fell after mandatory fortification
In-house exam

Diet low in folic acid, maternal obesity, excess alcohol and anticonvulsants (valproate, phenytoin, carbamazepine) are the tested NTD risk factors. MTHFR 677C>T → ↑ homocysteine.

Memory tool

Folic acid doses: "0.4 for everyone, 4 for the one who's had one."

Q3. How do oral clefts form, and how often do they recur?
  • Failure of the frontonasal (medial nasal) process to fuse with the maxillary process; lips close by ≈8 weeks and palate by ≈12 weeks
  • Incidence: 1/1,000 Caucasians, 1.7/1,000 Japanese, 0.4/1,000 African Americans
  • Concordance: MZ 35–40%, DZ 5%
  • Recurrence: 5% after one affected child, 8% after two
  • 85% isolated, 15% syndromic; managed by a craniofacial team (plastic surgery, ENT, audiology, speech, dental)

Facial processes

  • Upper lip forms by week 6 from medial nasal + maxillary processes

Genes (non-syndromic)

  • TGFA, TGFB3, IRF6, VAX1, FOXE1, MYH9, MAFB, ABCA4, BMP4, FGFR2, CRISPLD2, SUMO1, MTHFR, PVRL1, FGF8, PDGFC, GSTT1, MSX1

Prenatal exposures

  • Smoking (worse with maternal GSTT1 and infant MSX1 genotypes)
  • Alcohol; folic acid deficiency
  • Steroids; anticonvulsants (phenytoin, valproate)
High yield

Recurrence rises with each affected child (5% → 8%): a hallmark of multifactorial ("complex") inheritance that single-gene risks don't show.

Step 1

Cleft lip = failed fusion of maxillary + medial nasal processes (primary palate). Cleft palate = failed fusion of the palatine shelves (secondary palate).

LO 3Isolated vs syndromic defects

Q1. Why does it matter if a defect is part of a syndrome?
  • Isolated defects follow multifactorial recurrence (≈2–5%); syndromic defects follow the syndrome's rule (AR 25%, AD 50%, teratogen avoidable)
  • Clues: growth retardation, developmental delay, a cluster of minor anomalies, other major anomalies, family history across generations, a known exposure
  • Approach: listen to the family, take a full history, examine for minor anomalies (inner lower lip, palpebral fissures, philtrum, nails), get old records and photos, and reexamine when stuck
Memory tool

"Cleft + something else" or "NTD + something else" = look for a syndrome before quoting a multifactorial risk.

In-house exam

Cleft lip + mother with epilepsy on phenytoin → fetal hydantoin syndrome. Cleft + lower-lip pits across generations → van der Woude (IRF6, AD). Toddler with spina bifida, short palpebral fissures and thin philtrum → FAS (clinical diagnosis).

Trap — penetrance vs expressivity

van der Woude shows variable expressivity (lip pits only vs full cleft) with high penetrance. Pits can hide on the inner lower lip; a "normal" relative may be affected.

Step 1

Valproate → NTDs (spina bifida); carbamazepine → NTDs; phenytoin → fetal hydantoin; methotrexate → NTDs (antifolate). All anticonvulsant pregnancies get high-dose folic acid.

MCQExam-style questions

Score: 0 / 0

1Which category of genetic disease is most common in the population?

Multifactorial: 20–40%.

2A condition shows 38% concordance in MZ twins and 5% in DZ twins. Best interpretation?

MZ > DZ but <100% → genes + environment (oral cleft).

3Why might monozygotic twins differ for a genetic trait?

MZ twins share 100% of their starting genome, but post-zygotic changes diverge.

4A woman with epilepsy on valproate wants to conceive. Which birth defect is her baby at highest risk of, and what reduces it?

Valproate (antifolate effect) → NTDs; high-dose folic acid before conception.

5The MTHFR 677C>T variant raises NTD risk mainly by:

Reduced folate processing → homocysteine can't become methionine.

6A woman's previous pregnancy had a myelomeningocele. Recommended folic acid?

High risk: 4,000 µg/day, preconception through 1st trimester.

7When does the neural tube normally close?

Very early, often before pregnancy is recognized → supplement all women of reproductive age.

8A couple has had two children with isolated cleft lip and palate. Recurrence risk for the next child?

5% after one, 8% after two: risk rises with more affected relatives.

9A child has cleft lip, hypertelorism, a short nose and small, hypoplastic fingernails. His mother takes an anticonvulsant for epilepsy. Most likely diagnosis?

Phenytoin: cleft + distal phalanx/nail hypoplasia.

10A child with cleft lip has a father with lower-lip pits and missing teeth. Gene and inheritance?

van der Woude: 50% recurrence.

11A newborn has an encephalocele, polydactyly and polycystic kidneys. Recurrence risk for the parents?

Meckel-Gruber, AR.

12Both parents of a 30-year-old have type 2 diabetes. Her approximate lifetime risk?

One parent 40%, both parents 70%; lifestyle modification still matters.
